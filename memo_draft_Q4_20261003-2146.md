## Q4. The Relationship Between Mandamus and Justiciability

The authorities treat justiciability and mandamus as sequential rather than synonymous inquiries: justiciability determines whether the claim engages a legally enforceable obligation, and mandamus is the remedy that enforces such an obligation where it exists and where its own prerequisites are satisfied. The Supreme Court of Canada framed the threshold in exactly that order in Canada (Auditor General) v. Canada (Minister of Energy, Mines and Resources), [1989] 2 S.C.R. 49, asking "are the claims which the Auditor General seeks to invoke legally enforceable?", and then resolving the remedial question by holding that "s. 7(1)(b) of the Auditor General Act is the only remedy the Auditor General has with regard to the claimed denial of his s. 13(1) rights of access to information." The Court added that "[t]he appropriateness of an enlarged mandate for the Auditor General is for Parliament, not the courts, to decide." Where the only remedy is a report to the House, mandamus to compel access cannot lie. (No paragraph pinpoints are available for the quoted passages of that decision.)

### Justiciability as a precondition to mandatory relief

The clearest appellate statement of the conditioning rule is Canada v. Boloh 1(a), 2023 FCA 120, where the Federal Court of Appeal characterized the declarations that would have required the government to take steps to repatriate four detained citizens as "disguised mandatory orders or disguised mandamus remedies against the Government of Canada" (at para 60), and held that "[h]owever, mandatory obligations or mandamus cannot be imposed without first determining whether their exacting legal prerequisites are met" (at para 63). The Court also held that "[t]he Government of Canada is entitled to consider possible dangers and other considerations such as foreign relations, international affairs and national security" (at para 64). Boloh is framed in the language of the legal prerequisites of mandamus and of deference in foreign relations and national security rather than in the vocabulary of justiciability, but its practical effect is to close off mandatory relief in that class of case.

At the trial level the link between justiciability and the existence of a duty is made explicit in Uyghur Rights Advocacy Project v. Canada (Attorney General), 2023 FC 126. The Court there stated that "there are many cases where this Court has ordered mandamus requiring an agent or office or other representative of the government to act, but that must flow from a duty to act" (at para 39), and that "[h]ere, while there may be a duty for Canada to challenge the PRC's actions in an international forum, a duty to act in Canadian law can only be established by finding that this Court has jurisdiction to hear the matter" (at para 40). The same decision treats justiciability and jurisdiction together, stating that "[j]usticiability relates to a court's jurisdiction in the sense that a justiciable issue is one that does not exceed a court's jurisdiction by encroaching on the exclusive powers of the legislative or executive branches" (at para 59). The non-justiciability of the political questions raised therefore barred the relief sought, and the two doctrines were addressed as overlapping rather than as a single inquiry.

Friends of the Earth v. Canada (Governor in Council), 2008 FC 1183, approaches justiciability as a threshold question of statutory interpretation, holding that "[t]he KPIA either imposes the legal duties postulated by FOTE or it does not and no question of deference arises on that issue" (at para 19). The Court then tied the availability of a meaningful remedy to the same question: "[t]he argument that ss. 7(1) creates a justiciable duty is further weakened by the problem facing the Court for crafting a meaningful remedy" (at para 39), and "[i] question whether, outside of the constitutional context, the Court has any role to play in controlling or directing the other branches of government in the conduct of their legislative and regulatory functions" (at para 40). Independently of its justiciability holding, the Court stated that it would decline, as a matter of discretion, to grant a mandatory order even if it were wrong on justiciability [verify].

British Columbia Nurses' Union v. Attorney General of British Columbia, 2008 BCSC 321, links justiciability to the precision of the statutory duty relied on. Kelleher J. observed that "[t]he petitioner points to a precise piece of legislation that imposes a duty on the Commission and raises a justiciable issue" and that "the petitioner seeks a declaration that the Commission must enforce the law as it exists" (at para 29), and held that "[c]ompliance with the Act is not a matter of administrative discretion" and "It does not have a discretion to decide whether to enforce the Act" (at para 30). On mandamus specifically, the Court noted that "courts have left open the availability of mandamus to compel a public officer to exercise a duty" (at para 60) and that "[i]t cannot be determined at this stage that the court lacks jurisdiction to issue orders of mandamus or the declarations sought" (at para 69). The decision is a screening disposition under Rule 19(24), not a final ruling on the merits, and the same judgment denied the Union public interest standing.

Professional Association of Foreign Service Officers v. Canada (Treasury Board), 2026 FC 1136, treats justiciability as a preliminary issue going to the Court's jurisdiction, with the consequence that the alternative statutory forum could not supply the remedy: "[a]n arbitrator lacks the public law jurisdiction to issue a prerogative writ of mandamus or to supervise the systemic administration of a statutory scheme" (at para 43), and "[f]urthermore, the internal grievance mechanisms cannot provide adequate remedy for the Applicant and therefore the mandamus remains available" (at para 44); accordingly "neither the PSHPC Process, the NJC Process nor the FPSLRA Process provide adequate alternate forums available, and this Court retains its jurisdiction" (at para 55). A finding of justiciability and of jurisdiction did not, however, entitle the applicant to relief; the application was dismissed for want of evidence of the ongoing scope of the breach and of any practical value in an order [verify].

### Justiciability does not itself establish entitlement to mandamus

Canadian Association of Elizabeth Fry Societies v. Canada (Attorney General), 2026 FC 958, separates the two inquiries. The Court agreed with the applicant that "the issues raised in this application are justiciable; namely, the alleged failure of CSC to meet its statutory obligation to inmates pursuant to s. 70 of the CCRA" (at para 77), but it did so within its public interest standing analysis, stating that "if there is a legally enforceable duty, the applicants have made out the common law test for public interest standing, and there is no alternative recourse, mandamus may be appropriate" (at para 206). Mandamus was then assessed separately on the Apotex test: "[h]aving made out the first four criteria of the Apotex test, the Applicant has a prima facie entitlement to mandamus" (at para 211). The justiciability of the underlying issue was therefore a threshold matter that did not by itself establish entitlement to the writ.

Zaghbib v. Canada (Public Safety and Emergency Preparedness), 2016 FCA 182, is the leading appellate qualification on this point. Pelletier J.A. observed that "[t]his is not a case in which there are no objective criteria to apply or facts to be determined" (at para 27) and that "[n]or, in my view, is it self-evident that the exercise of the discretion as to when and how to launch an investigation is beyond judicial review" (at para 34). Justiciability accordingly did not, in itself, bar a mandamus application aimed at compelling an investigation. The appeal was nonetheless dismissed because the application had become moot once the CBSA decided and closed the file, the court declining to inquire into the merits unless the decision amounted to a disguised refusal to act [verify]. The disposition therefore rests on mootness — a distinct threshold doctrine — and not on justiciability.

SNC-Lavalin Group Inc. v. Canada (Public Prosecution Service), 2019 FC 282, reached a similar separation of issues by way of obiter. The Court stated that "[i]t is not necessary to address the question of whether mandamus would be available as a remedy for the Applicants on judicial review" (at para 174), because "[t]he Court notes that the availability of mandamus would also be determined by the same findings" (at para 175) — namely the findings that resolved the reviewability of the impugned decision. The Court added that mandamus "requires among other elements that there is a public duty to act" and that "mandamus cannot be used to compel the exercise of discretion in a particular way" (at para 175). Availability of the remedy thus follows from, but is not identical to, the antecedent reviewability analysis.

Page v. Mulcair, 2013 FC 402, illustrates the dependence of mandamus on a live controversy and an actual refusal. The Court declined to answer the referred questions because "[t]herefore, the questions are hypothetical and I decline to answer them on the grounds of non-justiciability" (at para 5). The Court recorded that counsel for Mr. Mulcair, while urging the Court to answer, "accepts that Mr. Page could have proceeded by way of declaration or mandamus" (at para 56) — a submission, not a holding. The decision also notes the holding, in the authority before it, that "the issue of mandamus would constitute an intrusion upon the privileges of the legislative assembly" (at para 38), which identifies legislative privilege as a further limit on the remedy.

### Directions in the nature of mandamus and the justiciability of discretion

The Supreme Court's most recent treatment is Ontario (Attorney General) v. Restoule, 2024 SCC 27, where the Court held that Crown discretion under the Robinson Treaties' augmentation clause "is not unfettered; it is justiciable and reviewable by the courts" (at para 196). The Court explained that "[a]lthough it is not the business of the courts to force the Crown to exercise its discretion in a particular way, it is very much the business of the courts to review exercises of Crown discretion for constitutional compliance", and that "[w]hile not a perfect analogy, a direction to this effect bears a family resemblance to an order in the nature of mandamus, insofar as it orders that discretion be exercised, without specifying exactly how" (at para 299). Justiciability of the discretion and compulsory relief are therefore linked, though the Court expressly qualifies the mandamus analogy as imperfect.

At the trial level, Hameed v. Canada (Prime Minister), 2024 FC 242, shows the two concepts arising together in the disposition of a mandamus claim without a single articulated rule joining them. The Federal Court disposed of the matter as follows: "[t]he Court grants the application in part. The request for an order of mandamus is dismissed, as is the request for the first declaration. However declarations will be issued." (at para 201). On justiciability the Court adopted the language of Conacher, stating that "[t]he hallmark of justiciability is whether the exercise of prerogative affects the rights or legitimate expectations of an individual" (at para 120), and held that "[t]he fact they may not be enforced at law is irrelevant in this case" (at para 110), in relation to constitutional conventions. The result is that declaratory relief was granted while mandamus was refused.

### Keeping the neighbouring doctrines distinct

The Federal Court of Appeal's decision in Canada (Prime Minister) v. Hameed, 2025 FCA 118, must be read as a jurisdiction case and not as a justiciability case. The respondent had sought "an order of mandamus or, in the alternative, a declaration pursuant to sections 18 and 18.1 of the Federal Courts Act" (at para 2); the Federal Court "dismissed the requested order for mandamus on the basis that neither the Prime Minister nor the Minister of Justice have a legal duty vis-à-vis the judicial appointment process" (at para 4). The Court of Appeal held that "[t]he nature and existence of that duty remains a live controversy, meaning this appeal is not moot" (at para 12) — a mootness ruling — and then disposed of the appeal on the statutory grant of jurisdiction: "I find that the Federal Court erred in concluding that sections 18 and 18.1 of the Federal Courts Act provided a statutory grant of jurisdiction" (at para 63), and "I would therefore allow the appeal and set aside the Federal Court's Decision (2024 FC 242) for lack of jurisdiction" (at para 65). Counsel should not recast that holding as a justiciability holding; it turns on the statutory grant of jurisdiction, while the legal-duty question was left as a live controversy and the mootness point was resolved against the Crown.

The same discipline applies to Uyghur, which — as noted above — treats justiciability and jurisdiction together as overlapping concepts (at para 59), and to Professional Association of Foreign Service Officers, which addresses justiciability, adequate alternative remedy and the retention of jurisdiction as separate steps (at paras 43, 44 and 55). Zaghbib turns on mootness, not on justiciability or jurisdiction. Elizabeth Fry treats justiciability within public interest standing and mandamus within the Apotex framework (at paras 77, 206 and 211). In no decision described above are these doctrines collapsed into one another.

### Weight of authority

The weight of authority favours the conditioning approach. The two Supreme Court of Canada decisions in the material point the same way: Auditor General requires that the claimed right be legally enforceable before the Court will intervene and identifies the statutory reporting mechanism as the only remedy, and Restoule confirms that Crown discretion may be justiciable and reviewable without the courts dictating how it is exercised, drawing an express (if imperfect) analogy to mandamus. At the appellate level, Boloh states the prerequisite rule directly (at para 63), while Zaghbib qualifies it only to the extent of holding that justiciability is not self-evidently a bar to a mandamus application directed at an investigation (at para 34) — a qualification that did not change the outcome, which rested on mootness. Hameed in the Federal Court of Appeal is a jurisdiction and mootness decision and does not disturb the conditioning approach. The trial-level authority — Uyghur, Friends of the Earth, British Columbia Nurses' Union, Elizabeth Fry, Professional Association of Foreign Service Officers and Hameed in the Federal Court — is uniformly consistent with the proposition that justiciability is a threshold condition for mandatory relief, while adding that a justiciability finding is neither necessary nor sufficient in itself to obtain mandamus. A contrary holding that justiciability is irrelevant to the availability of mandamus does not appear in the material.

### Gaps

No Supreme Court of Canada decision in the material sets out a general test for the relationship between justiciability and mandamus; the two Supreme Court authorities address the point in the specific contexts of a parliamentary reporting remedy and a treaty augmentation clause, and Restoule expressly labels its mandamus analogy imperfect. The Federal Court of Appeal has not, in the material provided, decided an appeal squarely on whether a justiciability finding is a precondition to mandamus, since Zaghbib was resolved on mootness and Hameed on the statutory grant of jurisdiction. The most direct statements of the relationship are accordingly trial-level, including two decisions from 2026, and should be advanced as persuasive rather than as settled appellate authority; the proposition that mandamus requires a legally enforceable duty is well supported, but the converse proposition — that a finding of justiciability is sufficient to ground the remedy — has no support in the material.
