## Q1. The Concept of Justiciability

Justiciability is not a question of a court's formal capacity to entertain a dispute, but of its proper place in the constitutional order. The foundational treatment remains *Operation Dismantle v. The Queen*, [1985] 1 SCR 441, where the Court divided its reasons but not its conception of the doctrine. Wilson J., in concurring reasons, framed the inquiry as one of "whether the courts should or must rather than on whether they can deal with such matters" (*Operation Dismantle v. The Queen*, [1985] 1 SCR 441 at para 54), and concluded that "[t]he government's decision to permit testing of the cruise missile in Canada cannot escape judicial review on any of the grounds advanced" (at para 109). Dickson J., writing the principal reasons, agreed "in substance with Madame Justice Wilson's discussion of justiciability and her conclusion that the doctrine is founded upon a concern with the appropriate role of the courts as the forum for the resolution of different types of disputes" (at para 38), and stated that he had "no doubt that disputes of a political or foreign policy nature may be properly cognizable by the courts" (at para 38). Wilson J. also separated factual weakness from non-justiciability: "[i]t might well be that, if the issue were allowed to go to trial, the appellants would lose simply by reason of their not having been able to establish the factual basis of their claim" (at para 51).

### The foundational conception: a normative inquiry into the courts' role

The Court consolidated that conception in *Canada (Auditor General) v. Canada (Minister of Energy, Mines and Resources)*, [1989] 2 SCR 49, describing justiciability as a doctrine "founded upon a concern with the appropriate role of the courts as the forum for the resolution of different types of disputes," and holding that "[a]n inquiry into justiciability is, first and foremost, a normative inquiry into the appropriateness as a matter of constitutional judicial policy of the courts deciding a given issue or, instead, deferring to other decision-making institutions of the polity." Two qualifications accompany that holding. First, in the non-Charter context Parliament may itself settle the question: "[i]f, then, the courts interpret a particular provision as having the effect of ousting judicial remedies for entitlements contained in that statute, they are, in principle, giving effect to Parliament's view of the justiciability of those rights." Second, the Court cautioned that "[t]he above analysis shall not be taken to detract from the fundamental principle that the courts should not readily decline to grant remedies for rights recognized by the laws of Canada."

The Court restated the doctrine in *Reference re Secession of Quebec*, [1998] 2 SCR 217, holding that "[t]he notion of justiciability is . . . linked to the notion of appropriate judicial restraint," and that on a reference the Court should not ask whether the dispute is adversarial or disposes of cognizable rights — "[n]o matter how closely the procedure on a reference may mirror the litigation process, a reference does not engage the Court in a disposition of rights" — but "whether the dispute is appropriately addressed by a court of law." It also recognized that a question with a "significant extralegal component" may be answered only as to its legal aspects. The Court's most recent general statement is *Highwood Congregation of Jehovah's Witnesses (Judicial Committee) v. Wall*, 2018 SCC 26, which defines the doctrine in terms of subject matter: "Justiciability relates to the subject matter of a dispute. The general question is this: Is the issue one that is appropriate for a court to decide?" (at para 32). *Highwood* also supplies the modern framework's flexibility: "There is no single set of rules delineating the scope of justiciability. Indeed, justiciability depends to some degree on context, and the proper approach to determining justiciability must be flexible" (at para 34).

### The governing test: purely political question versus sufficient legal component

Where a court is asked to decline to decide, the operative test is whether the matter is "purely political in nature" and should be determined elsewhere, or has "a sufficient legal component" to warrant judicial intervention. The Supreme Court of Canada stated the test in *Reference Re Canada Assistance Plan (B.C.)*, [1991] 2 SCR 525, which also confirms that a reference court "has a discretion to refuse to answer questions which are not justiciable," and that "[i]n exercising its discretion whether to determine a matter that is alleged to be non-justiciable, the Court's primary concern is to retain its proper role within the constitutional framework of our democratic form of government." Applying it, the Court found "both of the questions posed have a significant legal component" and that they "raise matters that are justiciable and should be answered." The same formula was carried into the *Secession Reference* ("the Court must determine whether the question is purely political in nature and should, therefore, be determined in another forum or whether it has a sufficient legal component") and into *Operation Dismantle*'s appellate progeny. In *Black v. Chretien*, C33887 (ONCA, 2001), the Ontario Court of Appeal put it this way: "At the core of the subject matter test is the notion of justiciability. The notion of justiciability is concerned with the appropriateness of courts deciding a particular issue, or instead deferring to other decision-making institutions like Parliament" (at para 50), the prerogative being "justiciable, or amenable to the judicial process, if its subject matter affects the rights or legitimate expectations of an individual" (at para 51). At the Federal Court of Appeal, the same framework appears in *Copello v. Canada (Minister of Foreign Affairs)*, 2003 FCA 295 at para 17: "The notion of justiciability is concerned with the appropriateness of courts deciding a particular issue, or instead deferring to other decision-making institutions like Parliament. . . . Only those exercises of the prerogative that are justiciable are reviewable."

The Ontario Court of Appeal's fullest application is *Tanudjaja v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2014 ONCA 852. For the majority, Pardu J.A. adopted the *Auditor General* formulation — "[a]n inquiry into justiciability is, first and foremost, a normative inquiry into the appropriateness as a matter of constitutional judicial policy of the courts deciding a given issue, or instead deferring to other decision making institutions of the polity" (at para 20) — and explained that political questions "must demonstrably be unsuitable for adjudication," typically because they "involve moral, strategic, ideological, historical or policy considerations that are not susceptible to resolution through adversarial presentation of evidence or the judicial process" (at para 21). The majority framed the issue as "one of institutional competence. The question is whether there is a sufficient legal component to anchor the analysis" (at para 35). Because the challenge was not to a specific law or state action, as in *PHS Community Services* or *Chaoulli*, "there is no sufficient legal component to engage the decision-making capacity of the courts" (at para 27), and the application was "demonstrably unsuitable for adjudication" (at para 36).

### Contextual, flexible and narrow: the modern appellate framework

The weight of appellate authority reads the doctrine narrowly. The Federal Court of Appeal's governing statement is *Hupacasath First Nation v. Canada (Foreign Affairs and International Trade Canada)*, 2015 FCA 4: "Justiciability, sometimes called the 'political questions objection,' concerns the appropriateness and ability of a court to deal with an issue before it" (at para 62); "[w]hether the question before the Court is justiciable bears no relation to the source of the government power" (at para 63); and "[t]hese cases show that the category of non-justiciable cases is very small" (at para 67). *Hupacasath* also held that assessing whether legal rights exist is itself the judicial function: "Assessing whether or not legal rights exist on the facts of a case lies at the core of what courts do. Under the constitutional separation of powers, determining this is squarely within our province. Canada's justiciability objection has no merit" (at para 70). It noted that "[t]here are authorities suggesting that executive decisions to sign a treaty, without more, are not justiciable" (at para 68), and accepted the "narrow proposition that Charter cases are justiciable regardless of the nature of the government action, be it an exercise of the Crown prerogative or otherwise" (at para 61). *Wenham v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2018 FCA 199, describes *Hupacasath* as "[t]he current governing authority in this Court on justiciability" (at para 59), repeats that justiciability "is best understood by the term used for it in the United States: the political questions objection" (at para 60), and holds that "[c]ases that are the normal grist for administrative law review — cases that raise issues of constitutionality legality, vires, reasonableness and procedural fairness based on administrative law authorities and settled doctrine — are almost always justiciable" (at para 61). *Wenham* also warns that "justiciability is different from deference and should not be confused with it" (at para 63).

The British Columbia Court of Appeal is to the same effect. *Nova-BioRubber Green Technologies Inc. v. Investment Agriculture Foundation British Columbia*, 2022 BCCA 247, states that "[t]here is a narrow category of decisions which are not amenable to judicial review because they raise issues that are non-justiciable" (at para 58), that "[t]he issue of justiciability is contextual. The court must consider whether it has the institutional capacity and legitimacy to adjudicate the matter: *Highwood* at para. 34" (at para 58), and that "[p]olicy decisions may be, and frequently are, subject to judicial review. There is no general bar on an application for judicial review of policy decisions" (at para 57). *La Rose v. Canada*, 2023 FCA 241, supplies the most systematic recent restatement: "[j]usticiability distinguishes claims suitable for judicial determination from those that are not" (at para 24); "[n]o firm criteria for assessing justiciability exist, and the boundaries between justiciable and non-justiciable matters are not always clear," and "[t]he issue often distills to a single question as to whether the claim has a sufficient legal component upon which a court can adjudicate" (at para 28). *La Rose* identifies the doctrine's two motives — "[t]he constitutional consideration is the court's respect for its role in a Westminster parliamentary democracy" (at para 26) and "[t]he pragmatic consideration arises from the limitations on a court's ability to fashion and implement remedies. This is a component of the institutional limitation" (at para 27) — and holds that "[t]he fact that the matter is complex, contentious or laden with social values does not mean that the courts can abdicate the responsibility vested in them by our Constitution to review legislation for Charter compliance when citizens challenge it" (at para 29). *Mathur v. Ontario*, 2024 ONCA 762, likewise affirms that "the Constitution requires that courts review legislation and state action for Charter compliance when citizens challenge them, even when the issues are complex, contentious and laden with social values" (at para 36), while recording that the application judge had found the Charter issues justiciable but had "agreed with Ontario that the court did not have institutional capacity and legitimacy to determine Canada and Ontario's 'fair share' of the remaining carbon budget" (at para 25), and that "Ontario argues that the central issue raised on this appeal is effectively how to plan to address the future adverse effects of global climate change, which is not a justiciable question" (at para 30).

### The legal-rights threshold in judicial review

A distinct and frequently decisive formulation requires that the impugned conduct affect legal rights. The Federal Court of Appeal applied it in *Democracy Watch v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2021 FCA 133: "This Court has found that if the conduct attacked in an application for judicial review fails to affect legal rights, impose legal obligations, or cause prejudicial effects, there is no right to bring a judicial review" (at para 23); "[t]he criteria to find that the matter is justiciable, as affirmed in *Democracy Watch 2009*, must be applied here. A matter that fails to affect legal rights, impose legal obligations, or cause prejudicial effects is not reviewable" (at para 29); and "the issue raised in this application is not justiciable because it does not affect rights, impose legal obligations or cause prejudicial effects" (at para 44). *Air Passenger Rights v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2024 FCA 128, applies the same threshold to administrative conduct short of a formal decision, noting that a "[m]atter" "is not restricted to a decision or order" and "extends to 'any matter in respect of which a remedy may be available' under section 18 of the FC Act" (at para 16), and holding that "for a remedy to be available a matter must 'affect legal rights, impose legal obligations, or cause prejudicial effects'" (at para 44), the impugned Statement not being justiciable (at para 31).

The Supreme Court of Canada adopted a parallel approach to voluntary associations in *Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church of Canada St. Mary Cathedral v. Aga*, 2021 SCC 22: "Courts have jurisdiction to intervene in decisions of voluntary associations only where a legal right is affected" (at para 27); "[w]here there is no contract, or other obligation known to law, there is no justiciable interest and no cause of action" (at para 24); and "[t]he legal rights which can ground jurisdiction include private rights — rights in property, contract, tort or unjust enrichment — and statutory causes of action" (at para 29).

### Illustrations of the boundary

The clearest non-justiciable categories in the authorities are religious doctrine, the honours prerogative, and the legislative process. In *Highwood*, the Court held that "[s]ecular judicial determinations of theological or religious disputes, or of contentious matters of religious doctrine, unjustifiably entangle the court in the affairs of religion" (at para 36), and that "the courts will not consider the merits of a religious tenet; such matters are not justiciable" (at para 37). In *Black*, the Ontario Court of Appeal concluded that "Prime Minister Chrétien's exercise of the honours prerogative by giving advice to the Queen about granting Mr. Black's peerage is not justiciable and therefore not judicially reviewable" (at para 64). As to the legislative process, the majority in *Mikisew Cree First Nation v. Canada (Governor General in Council)*, 2018 SCC 40, held that "[t]wo constitutional principles — the separation of powers and parliamentary sovereignty — dictate that it is rarely appropriate for courts to scrutinize the law-making process" (at para 2), the question being "whether the honour of the Crown gives rise to a justiciable duty to consult when ministers develop legislation that could adversely affect the Mikisew's treaty rights" (at para 29). The Federal Court of Appeal majority in *Canada (Governor General in Council) v. Mikisew Cree First Nation*, 2016 FCA 311, reasoned that "legislative action is not a proper subject for an application for judicial review under the Federal Courts Act" (at para 3), that "this matter has been expressly left open by the Supreme Court in *Rio Tinto*" (at para 47), that "courts will not supervise the legislative process and will provide no relief until a bill has been enacted" (at para 59), and that imposing a duty to consult during the process "would not only be impractical and cumbersome and potentially grind the legislative process to a halt" (at para 60). Pelletier J.A., concurring, considered the claim justiciable in any event as a claim for relief against the Crown under s. 17 of the *Federal Courts Act*, while concluding that no duty to consult attaches to laws of general application [verify].

Foreign relations supply a further illustration, though one now qualified. In *Copello*, the Federal Court of Appeal held that "[i]n general, the exercise of Crown prerogative is beyond the scope of judicial review, except, of course, when a right guaranteed by the Canadian Charter" is engaged (at para 16), and that the usual administrative-law rules did not apply to the recall of a diplomat (at para 22). In *Democracy Watch v. British Columbia (Lieutenant Governor)*, 2023 BCCA 404, the court held that the chambers judge did not err "in concluding that the Premier's advice on dissolution is non-justiciable" (at para 29), applying *Highwood*'s framework — reiterating that "[t]here is no single set of rules delineating the scope of justiciability" and that "[t]he court should ask whether it has the institutional capacity and legitimacy to adjudicate the matter" (at para 73) — and declining to apply the United Kingdom prorogation case: "I do not consider *Miller* to assist in the analysis required in the circumstances of this case" (at para 84). The appellants in that case had relied on *Operation Dismantle*, the *Secession Reference* and *Canada Assistance Plan* for the proposition that Canadian courts may engage political controversy where a question of legality is raised; that reliance is recorded as their submission, not as the court's holding.

At the other end, courts have found matters justiciable where a legal component or individual impact is present. *Canada Christian College and School of Graduate Theological Studies v. Post-Secondary Education Quality Assessment Board*, 2023 ONCA 544, holds that "[t]here is no question that the Minister's decisions under review are justiciable" and that "[w]hether those decisions are characterized as legislative, administrative or adjudicative in nature may be relevant to the substance of the judicial review analysis . . . but such characterization does not affect their justiciability" (at para 29). *Sharif v. Canada (Public Safety and Emergency Preparedness)*, 2026 ONCA 476, describes justiciability as existing "on a sliding scale" (at para 97) and holds that "matters that directly implicate the rights or legitimate expectations of individuals are reviewable given their direct impact on those individuals: *Black*, at para. 51" (at para 98); the Minister's refusal to hold removal in abeyance was therefore "justiciable as, although it arises in the context of international relations, it directly and profoundly affects the rights, safety, and life of a specific individual" (at para 100). The court declined to treat *Ahani* as controlling, observing that "this court did not specifically address the issue of justiciability or decide that Mr. Ahani's claim was not justiciable" (at para 96).

Two further limits merit note. First, a bare limitation of a right is not enough: *Grain Farmers of Ontario v. Ontario (Environment and Climate Change)*, 2016 ONCA 283, holds that "[t]he limitation of a right does not, standing alone, create a justiciable issue" (at para 15); "the party seeking the declaration must establish that the question it raises in its application is a legal or justiciable issue" (at para 16); and "neither the wisdom nor the efficacy of a regulation is a justiciable issue" (at para 19). Second, ripeness operates as a companion constraint on declaratory relief: *Marmora and Lake (Municipality) v. Ontario*, 2025 ONCA 10, states that "courts will assume jurisdiction of a matter only when it becomes 'ripe' for judicial determination, in the sense that there is a live controversy, with a sufficient factual foundation, and no other prior, procedural avenues" (at para 9), and upheld the application judge's conclusion that the broad relief sought, being "far broader and more complex than the orders sought in *Schaeffer*," rested on an inadequate evidentiary record (at para 17), her ripeness conclusion disclosing no legal error, material misapprehension of evidence or unreasonableness (at para 18).

### Divergence and qualification

The weight of authority runs strongly in favour of a narrow, contextual doctrine. Every Supreme Court decision bearing on the question — *Operation Dismantle*, *Auditor General*, *Canada Assistance Plan*, the *Secession Reference*, *Highwood*, *Mikisew Cree* and *Aga* — treats justiciability as an exception grounded in the courts' constitutional role, and both the Federal Court of Appeal (*Hupacasath*, *Wenham*, *La Rose*, *Democracy Watch 2021*, *Air Passenger Rights*) and the Ontario and British Columbia Courts of Appeal (*Black*, *Grain Farmers*, *Canada Christian College*, *Marmora*, *Mathur*, *Sharif*, *Nova-BioRubber*, *Democracy Watch BCCA*) have confined it accordingly.

The genuine divergences are nonetheless material for an intervention. *Tanudjaja* is the clearest: Feldman J.A., dissenting, would not have struck the claim at the pleadings stage, reasoning that "[f]or the moment, the justiciability of social and economic rights under the Charter remains an open question" (at para 79) and that "to strike a serious Charter application at the pleadings stage on the basis of justiciability is therefore inappropriate" (at para 81); the majority's contrary holding, resting on institutional competence (at paras 27, 35–36), is the operative authority. *Mathur* qualifies the *Tanudjaja* majority's reach by affirming Charter justiciability while leaving open the application judge's institutional-capacity concern about determining a "fair share" of the carbon budget. *Chiasson v. Canada*, 2003 FCA 155, cuts the other way on honours and prerogative: describing the doctrine as rendering a question "normally considered non-justiciable if there are no objective legal criteria to apply or no facts to be determined to decide the question," or if "some other branch of government is conspicuously more appropriate, in our constitutional system, to decide the matter" (at para 8), the Court declined to apply *Black* at the pleading stage, holding that "such a motion to strike should be rejected unless it is plain and obvious that the proceeding has no possibility of success" (at para 6), so that the action "cannot be dismissed at this stage on the grounds that it is plain and obvious that the subject matter is non-justiciable" (at para 17). The distinction between *Black* and *Chiasson* is thus procedural posture and the presence of a regulatory scheme rather than the governing conception of justiciability. Finally, *Sharif* and *Copello* must be read together: the same court that treated the recall of a diplomat as beyond review in *Copello* held in *Sharif* that a decision arising in international relations was justiciable because of its direct and profound impact on an individual's rights, safety and life (at para 100).

### Gaps

Two points on which the authorities are thin should be flagged for counsel. First, the interaction between the institutional-capacity branch of *La Rose* and *Mathur* and the rights-based threshold applied in *Democracy Watch 2021*, *Air Passenger Rights* and *Aga* is not squarely addressed in any decision available here; counsel should be prepared for the Court to treat the two lines as one framework or as two distinct screening devices, and should note that no appellate authority in this set resolves which prevails where conduct affects no legal right but is alleged to raise a sufficient legal component. Second, the parties' competing positions on treaty-making and dissolution — the "authorities suggesting that executive decisions to sign a treaty, without more, are not justiciable" noted in *Hupacasath* at para 68, and the appellants' reliance on *Operation Dismantle*, the *Secession Reference* and *Canada Assistance Plan* recorded in *Democracy Watch BCCA* — rest on submissions and provisional observations rather than on any holding in the material here, and should be advanced as such.
