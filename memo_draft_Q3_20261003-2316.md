## Q3. Control of Own Process, Caseload and Resources

The proposition that a tribunal's control over its own process, its caseload or its staffing resources renders its decisions — including delay — non-justiciable or immune from mandamus is not the footing on which the decisions available are decided. The weight of authority runs strongly against it. Where courts have relied on caseload, resources or process concerns to refuse relief, they have done so under the Apotex prerequisites for mandamus, as an exercise of the discretion to refuse the writ, on the basis that no public legal duty is owed, or on distinct threshold doctrines such as prematurity, mootness or standing. Only two of the decisions provided come close to supporting the proposition in its resource dimension, and both are first-instance decisions in regulatory enforcement and immigration processing rather than decisions about a tribunal's governance of its own proceedings. A genuine divergence of appellate and first-instance authority exists, not on justiciability, but on whether caseload and resource evidence can satisfactorily justify delay under the mandamus framework.

### The Supreme Court: resource use is part of the justiciable delay analysis, and mandamus remains available

In Law Society of Saskatchewan v. Abrametz, 2022 SCC 29, the majority reasons of Rowe J. treat both questions as justiciable and remedial. At para 64 the majority states: "Finally, whether the administrative body used its resources efficiently should be considered in the analysis of inordinate delay. That said, insufficient agency resources cannot excuse inordinate delay in any case:". Resource use is thus an analytical factor, and insufficiency of resources is expressly not an excuse. At para 80 the majority states that "[m]andamus can be sought to compel administrative decision makers to carry out their duties and, in so doing, to limit delay in administrative proceedings:", while at para 81 it adds that "[m]y comments here do not intend to change any of the standards applicable for obtaining an order of mandamus generally. They merely affirm that it may also be an appropriate tool to prevent and address abuse of process." That reservation at para 81 matters: it preserves the general standards for obtaining mandamus, including the discretionary criteria on which the resource and caseload arguments in the first-instance decisions find their foothold.

### Appellate authority

The most direct appellate rejection of the proposition is Benison v. Canada (Royal Canadian Mounted Police External Review Committee), 2026 FCA 53. The Court held, at para 94, that "[f]actors such as a sudden increase in caseload, a lengthy, albeit temporary dearth in resources and a prioritization system established to respond to the resulting backlog do not describe delays that are inherent in the appeals reviewed by the ERC," and, at para 95, that "[i]t is for the administrative decision-maker, not the appellants, to provide a satisfactory justification for delays that exceed the inherent time requirements of a matter, including delays based on the complexity of a specific case." The Court placed the onus on the tribunal and warned, at para 99, that the backlog explanation "is not tied to the delay actually experienced by the appellants and could thus serve to justify any administrative delay, however egregious." The Court allowed the appeal in part and directed the ERC to complete the appeals within a fixed period [verify].

To the same effect at the appellate level, though not framed as a resources question, is Morris v. British Columbia (Workers' Compensation Board), 2020 BCCA 293, where Saunders J.A. stated at para 10: "I accept that the Board has a public law duty to render a timely decision once it received a completed application. However, damages and other forms of relief are not recoverable from the court following inordinate delay. The proper remedy for delay is an order for mandamus, compelling the public body to render a decision", and at para 11: "Where, as here, a worker has a complaint that a statutory decision maker has failed to discharge its responsibilities to decide an application, the worker's remedy is to ask the court to direct the decision maker to fulfill its duty."

Two appellate decisions qualify rather than contradict, and both must be read in their own doctrinal terms. In Charette v. Commissioner of Competition, 2003 FCA 426, Sexton J.A. upheld the refusal of mandamus on discretionary grounds: the Commissioner "has the discretion to discontinue this inquiry at any time if he is of the opinion that the matter does not justify further inquiry" (at para 63); "[t]he Commissioner does not have unlimited resources, and if this Court were to order the Commissioner to hold a formal inquiry into matters which it had already thoroughly investigated, the Commissioner would have less resources to consider new complaints" (at para 67); and the repeated re-filing of the same complaint "constitutes an abuse of process of the Act, which also suggests that mandamus should not be granted in this case" (at para 68). The Court also upheld the finding below that "the balance of convenience is in favour of not granting the order of mandamus" (at para 62). This is a discretionary and abuse-of-process basis, built on statutory discretion; it is not a justiciability holding. Similarly, in Bernard v. Canada (Attorney General), 2018 FCA 23, the Court disposed of the mandamus request on mootness — "[a]fter the application was made, the Board released its decision and so, as the applicant admitted during oral argument, the request for mandamus is now moot" (at para 6) — and declined declaratory relief as a matter of discretion: "We consider that no purpose would be served in issuing a declaration concerning the Board's delay in this matter" (at para 7). Far from treating resources as an excuse, the Court stated that "[t]he Board's delay is regrettable and we consider that its determination of the applicant's complaint should have happened sooner," and that "[t]o the extent the delay was caused by inadequate resources and to the extent the Board needs more resources to fulfil its statutory mandate on a timely basis, it should demand them" (at para 7).

### First-instance authority treating delay as justiciable and resources as no answer

A substantial body of Federal Court authority proceeds on the footing that administrative delay is reviewable and remediable, and that workload or resource constraints do not excuse it. In Dragan v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2003 FCT 211, the Court held at para 58 that the respondent's demonstration of "the enormous workload faced by the Department ... cannot be used in this case as an excuse for their delay," and that "there is ample precedent to grant mandamus for the assessment of visa applications where the excuse for the delay is the enormous workload faced the by [sic] immigration department"; it also found an implied duty "to use his reasonable best efforts" to assess the applications (at para 60). In Singh v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2005 FC 544, the Court stated at para 16 that "[i]t matters not whether the delays lie within the Minister's office, or with CSIS. The Minister had a duty to act with reasonable diligence, taking into account that resources may be limited," adding that "Queues are a fact of life, but at this point in time, the delays are simply unacceptable," and, at para 3, that "one cannot investigate indefinitely. To do so is to refuse to make a decision at all." In I.P.P. v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2018 FC 123, the Court identified mandamus as "the obvious remedy" for delay (at para 316), noted that the applicants "did not seek the obvious solutions of complaining to the RPD or coming to the Court for mandamus if they thought the Member was taking too long to produce a decision" (at para 321), and drew "[a] negative inference from the Member's failure to adequately explain the delay in producing the Decision and his resort to vagaries" (at para 320). In Thomas v. Canada (Public Safety and Emergency Preparedness), 2020 FC 164, the Court held at para 7 that "[t]he administrative considerations advanced on behalf of the Minister are similar to ones that have repeatedly been held by this Court to be insufficient to justify exorbitant delay," and at para 30 that the Minister was "essentially reiterating the administrative constraints that have repeatedly been found by this Court to provide insufficient justification for exorbitant delay." In Almuhtadi v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2021 FC 712, the Court held at para 41 that "[t]he Minister had a duty to act with reasonable diligence, taking into account that resources may be limited. That duty is not satisfied simply by a delegation to CSIS, which falls within the purview of another Minister." In Jahantigh v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2023 FC 1253, the Court stated at para 19 that "[t]his Court has been clear that simply referring to a security review cannot justify any delay, regardless of its length or necessity," at para 20 that "[f]or the Court to assess whether the length of a security review is reasonable, it must have some information about the review and the reasons for its length," and at para 23 that the Minister's duty to act with reasonable diligence is not displaced by limited resources; the Court also dealt with partial mootness and ordered periodic status reporting [verify]. In Kia v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2026 FC 780, the Court held at para 27 that it was "not prepared to accept that the continuing delay relates to security screening as opposed to the misallocation or inefficient use of resources," and at para 29 that the respondent had "failed to discharge its onus to provide a justification for the delay," against the framework described at paras 24–25 that inordinate delay is "another form of administrative unreasonableness or unfairness, remedied by the specifically tailored relief of mandamus." In Roghangar v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2026 FC 987, the Court rejected reliance on volume and inferred complexity: "[t]he Respondent filed no evidence about the volume of security screenings" (at para 31), and "[i]n effect, the Respondent asks the Court to infer from the lengthy delay itself that the application must be complex or non-routine, and then to treat that inferred complexity as justification for the delay. I decline to do so" (at para 32).

### First-instance authority in which caseload and resource evidence defeated mandamus

A competing line applies the Apotex framework and accepts caseload and resource evidence as satisfactorily justifying delay. In Bedard v. Canada (Royal Canadian Mounted Police), 2024 FC 570, the Court found that "the evidence attributes the delays to increased caseload combined with staffing and administrative shortages" (at para 43), that the applicants had "failed to establish the ERC's delay in resolving their appeals has been longer than the nature of the process required, prima facie" (at para 60), and that "such evidence of caseload volume has been accepted as justified delay: Jia at para 90; Vaziri at para 57; and Abrametz at para 64" (at para 65), the ERC addressing its backlog "in a 'methodical and principled manner'" (at para 71). That approach was found to be a reviewable error on appeal: in Benison, 2026 FCA 53 at para 99, the Court held that the explanation accepted by the Federal Court was not tied to the delay actually experienced and "could thus serve to justify any administrative delay, however egregious." The same reasoning appears in Mazarei v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2014 FC 322, where the respondent provided "ample explanation for the delay" through the closure of the Damascus embassy (at para 25) and staff increases in Ankara (at para 26); in Yim v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2026 FC 254, where the Ministerial Instructions established "a priority sequence for the processing of SUV Program applications" (at para 28), the file had "not yet reached ... in the queue" (at para 29), and mandamus would "circumvent, and indeed repudiate, the processing priorities established in the Ministerial Instructions" (at para 31); and in Jin v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2026 FC 1080, where it "was reasonable and justified for the Respondent to shift its resources" (at para 25) and the delay was "justified and reasonably explained by the Respondent's decision to prioritize" other applications (at para 27). To the same effect in the discretionary register are Charette v. Canada (Commissioner of Competition), 2002 FCT 698, where the Court stated that it would not have exercised its discretion to grant relief (at para 23) and that "the balance of convenience does not favour the issuing of a mandamus order as the administrative cost that would follow is obvious and unacceptable," which "would monopolize Bureau resources to the detriment of the public interest in the effective operation of the Act" (at para 24), and Tumarkin v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2014 FC 915, where the absence of evidence "that the average is created by a malfunctioning under-resourced system" (at para 18) and the discretionary character of file splitting — "[w]hether a file should be split is a matter of discretion rather than of right. It is therefore not amenable to mandamus" (at para 22) — were material.

Critically, these decisions are framed in the language of the mandamus prerequisites and of discretion, not of justiciability. That is the distinction on which the response to the question should turn.

### The decisions closest to the proposition, and their limits

Canadian Horse Defense Coalition v. Canada (Food Inspection Agency), 2019 FC 1559 is the strongest supporting authority, but it rests on the absence of a public legal duty and on statutory enforcement discretion: "In my view, the CFIA has no public legal duty to the Coalition to enforce the HAR; if anything, its enforcement and regulatory duties are to the Minister" (at para 40); the Court adopted the principle that "[o]nly he who is charged with such public duty can determine how to utilize his resources. This is not a case where the Minister has turned his back on his duties, or where negligence or bad faith has been demonstrated" (at para 45); the Minister "has the discretion to choose how to 'tailor the means to the end'" (at para 46); and "[i]t is a case where they have clear discretion as to enforcement of the HAA and HAR, this Court should not interfere or intervene" (at para 50). That is a duty-and-discretion holding about a regulator's enforcement choices, and the matter was resolved on that footing rather than by declining to adjudicate.

Wang v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration), 2025 FC 1832 is the second supporting decision, and again it is a merits application of the mandamus framework: the delay was "satisfactorily justified" by "an established and orderly process" (at para 27), "[h]igh immigration levels generally constitute a reasonable explanation for delay in processing applications and does not justify issuing a writ of mandamus for any particular applicant impacted by the delay" (at para 29), and there was "no evidence that the time taken by the Minister in processing the Applicants' PR applications was due to bad faith, negligence or refusal to perform their duty" (at para 31). It concerns queue management in immigration processing, not a tribunal's control of its own proceedings.

### Where the related doctrines actually lie

The single closest textual statement to the proposition is in Nadeau v. Canada (Attorney General), 2019 FC 1367, where the Court said that "[t]he General Division is master of its own procedure and has full authority to determine the required evidence relevant to the exercise of its jurisdiction" (at para 7). On the passages provided, however, that statement is made in support of a prematurity and exhaustion-of-remedies conclusion: the proceeding was "an administrative proceeding governed by the Social Security Tribunal Regulations, SOR/2013-60, which has not been exhausted," rendering the application "premature" (at para 8), with the result "plain and obvious" (at para 11). That is a distinct threshold doctrine, and it should not be pleaded as a justiciability or resources holding.

Mikail v. Canada (Attorney General), 2011 FC 674 points the other way on tribunal control of process. Noël J. observed, at para 39, that "it matters little whether a tribunal chooses to formally suspend a proceeding or adjourn it sine die; form must not be elevated over substance," and that "in the event that the stay of proceedings ordered by SIRC is set aside, the Committee will be obliged to proceed with its investigation without it being necessary for the applicant to seek a mandamus," adding at para 40 that a tribunal's decisions within its investigations are reviewable — the latter passage recording the implication drawn from Justice de Montigny's reasons rather than a fresh holding of the Court. The direction of that decision is that the exercise of a tribunal's procedural powers remains subject to review.

Bernard, 2018 FCA 23 and Jahantigh, 2023 FC 1253 also engage mootness, and British Columbia Nurses' Union v. Attorney General of British Columbia, 2008 BCSC 321 was dismissed because the Union was denied public interest standing [verify]. On the passages provided in that case, Kelleher J. nonetheless rejected the premise that a commission's enforcement choices are discretionary in the relevant sense: "[c]ompliance with the Act is not a matter of administrative discretion," and the Commission "[d]oes not have a discretion to decide whether to enforce the Act" (at para 30). That reasoning cuts against the proposition, and it also illustrates the need to keep standing, prematurity, mootness and the merits of mandamus analytically separate from justiciability.

Weighing the authorities: Abrametz in the Supreme Court and Benison and Morris in the Federal Court of Appeal and the British Columbia Court of Appeal place the burden of justifying delay on the decision-maker and reject caseload, resource and prioritization explanations as inherent time requirements; the bulk of Federal Court authority grants mandamus notwithstanding workload and resource explanations. The supporting side consists of a first-instance enforcement-discretion decision (Canadian Horse Defense Coalition) and a first-instance immigration processing decision (Wang), together with the Apotex-stage decisions (Bedard, Mazarei, Yim, Jin, Charette (F.C.), Tumarkin), whose approach to justification was held to be a reviewable error in Benison. In practical terms, the proposition should be advanced, if at all, as a submission about the discretionary refusal of mandamus and the sufficiency of justification under Apotex, not as a submission that tribunal delay is non-justiciable.

### Gaps

The supporting side of the proposition is thin: it rests on first-instance decisions decided on enforcement discretion, absence of public legal duty, or the Apotex justification stage, and the most developed of the contrary first-instance reasoning — Bedard — was displaced on appeal in Benison. There is no appellate or Supreme Court authority holding that a tribunal's control of its own process, caseload or resources places its decisions or its delay beyond justiciability or beyond mandamus, and the treatment of Abrametz at para 64 diverges as between Bedard (2024 FC 570 at para 65) and Benison (2026 FCA 53 at paras 94–99), a divergence counsel should be prepared to address.
