## 1. *Yadeta v. Peel (Municipality) Police Service Board*, 2024 ONCA 341
**CanLII:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/22329/index.do

FACTS: The appellant, Banti Yadeta, was criminally charged with offences including receiving a material benefit from sexual services provided by a minor and making or publishing child pornography, after a complainant—who was underage and working as a sex worker—identified him to police as her pimp (paras 1-2). He was detained at the Maplehurst Correctional Complex following his arrest and again for approximately two months when he had no surety (para 3). At trial, after the complainant struggled to give evidence during cross-examination, the proceedings were resolved by the appellant entering into a peace bond and forfeiting nude photographs of the complainant, with the Crown withdrawing the charges (para 3). The appellant then brought a civil claim against the Peel Police, Maplehurst Correctional Complex, His Majesty the King, and the Attorney General of Ontario, alleging malicious prosecution, bad faith, negligence, and assault by another inmate (para 4). The motion judge dismissed his motion for leave under s. 17 of the *Crown Liability and Proceedings Act, 2019* and granted the respondents' motion to strike the claim under r. 21 of the *Rules of Civil Procedure* (paras 5-6).

ISSUES: The appeal raised whether the motion judge erred in refusing to grant leave under s. 17 of the *Crown Liability and Proceedings Act, 2019* to pursue claims for malicious prosecution, misfeasance in public office, and bad faith against the Crown (paras 6-7). The court also considered whether the motion judge erred in striking the claim under r. 21 for disclosing no reasonable cause of action, and whether leave to amend the claim should have been granted (paras 6-7, 11).

DECISION: The Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal, finding no error in the motion judge's decision (paras 8, 13). The court upheld the dismissal of the appellant's motion for leave under s. 17 of the *Crown Liability and Proceedings Act* and the striking of the claim, agreeing that the appellant failed to meet the statutory requirements and pleaded no material facts supporting his claims (paras 9-10). The respondents were awarded $5,000 each in costs, totalling $10,000 inclusive of disbursements and H.S.T. (para 14).

REASONS: The court held that the motion judge "correctly canvassed the statutory requirements to grant leave under s. 17" and properly found that "even on a generous reading of the appellant's claim and materials, there is no reasonable possibility that he could succeed" (para 9). The court emphasized that the appellant "failed to provide any material facts or evidence that support these claims," and that it is insufficient to merely state that material facts will be provided after discoveries (para 9). Critically, the court noted that the criminal proceedings "were not resolved in the appellant's favour as required for a claim of malicious prosecution," because the appellant "voluntarily entered into a peace bond and forfeited the nude images of the complainant in exchange for the withdrawal of the charges" (para 9). On the r. 21 motion, the court agreed that the appellant "has not pleaded any material facts in support of his claim against the Peel Police," nor did his claim negate reasonable and probable grounds for arrest given his own pleading that the complainant identified him as her pimp (para 10). The court also found no basis to interfere with the discretionary refusal to grant leave to amend, as the appellant had "several opportunities to amend his claim" and there was "no basis for anticipating that further amendments could cure the fatal defects" (para 11).

SIGNIFICANCE: This case reinforces the strict pleading requirements for civil claims against police and Crown defendants, particularly the necessity of pleading material facts and the requirement that criminal proceedings terminate in the plaintiff's favour to sustain a malicious prosecution claim. It also confirms that entering into a peace bond in exchange for withdrawal of charges does not constitute a favourable termination for the purposes of a malicious prosecution action.

## 2. *Theratechnologies Inc. v. 121851 Canada Inc.*, 2015 SCC 18
**CanLII:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/15290/index.do

FACTS: Theratechnologies inc. (Thera), a Montréal-based pharmaceutical company listed on the Toronto Stock Exchange, filed a new drug application with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) on June 1, 2009 for tesamorelin, a drug to reduce excess abdominal fat among HIV patients (para 6). As part of the FDA's routine approval process, the FDA referred questions about the drug's potential side effects to an expert Advisory Committee and published briefing materials on its website on May 25, 2010 (paras 7 and 13). When stock quotation enterprises publicized the FDA's questions about a possible diabetes risk, Thera's share price dropped 58 percent over two days; 121851 Canada inc., a holding company with 190,000 Thera shares, sold its shares during this period at a net loss of $271,752 (paras 14-16). The Advisory Committee unanimously recommended approval on May 27, 2010, the share price recovered, and the FDA ultimately approved the drug on November 10, 2010 (para 16). 121851 sought authorization under s. 225.4 of the Securities Act to bring a class action for damages, alleging that the FDA's questions and the side-effect information amounted to a material change triggering timely disclosure obligations under s. 73 (para 17).

ISSUES: The central issue was the proper interpretation of the "reasonable possibility" threshold in s. 225.4 of the Securities Act, which permits a court to grant authorization for a damages action "if it deems that the action is in good faith and there is a reasonable possibility that it will be resolved in favour of the plaintiff" (paras 3 and 17). The Court had to determine whether this threshold is more stringent than the general class action authorization standard under art. 1003 of the Code of Civil Procedure, and whether the evidentiary record established a reasonable possibility that 121851's action under s. 73 could succeed (paras 3 and 21).

DECISION: The appeal was allowed (para 4). The Court held that while the s. 225.4 threshold requires more than a mere possibility of success, the threshold was not met in this case because there was no reasonable possibility that 121851's action could succeed (paras 4 and 56). The Court set aside the authorization granted by the Motions Judge and affirmed by the Court of Appeal (paras 18-20 and 56).

REASONS: The Court held that the "reasonable possibility" standard in s. 225.4 sets out "a different and higher standard" than the general threshold for class action authorization under art. 1003 of the Code of Civil Procedure, requiring "a reasonable or realistic chance that the action will succeed" (paras 35-38). The Quebec legislature deliberately used different language to create "a more meaningful screening mechanism in the securities context so that costly strike suits and unmeritorious claims would be prevented" (para 38). While the claimant must offer "some credible evidence in support of the claim," the authorization stage "should not be treated as a mini-trial," and a full analysis of the evidence is unnecessary (paras 39-40). On the merits, the Court found that 121851 "has not pointed to any evidence that could qualify as a change in Thera's operations, capital or business as described in s. 5.3 of the Securities Act," since the clinical trial results, including potential side effects, "were disclosed to shareholders as they became available" (para 48). The Court further held that the FDA's questions to its Advisory Committee were "a routine step in the FDA's work to determine whether a drug's benefits outweigh its risks" and could not be characterized as "any kind of change to Thera's business, operations, or capital requiring a reassuring public response" (paras 49-50). Because the evidence did not "credibly point to a material change that could have triggered disclosure obligations," there was no reasonable possibility that the action could succeed (para 56).

SIGNIFICANCE: This case establishes that the s. 225.4 authorization threshold for secondary market securities class actions in Quebec is a more robust screening mechanism than the general class action authorization standard, requiring a "reasonable or realistic chance" of success supported by credible evidence, while stopping short of a mini-trial. It clarifies the two components of a "material change" under s. 5.3 of the Securities Act and confirms that routine regulatory steps in a drug approval process do not, without more, trigger timely disclosure obligations.

## 3. *Bayens v. Kinross Gold Corporation*, 2014 ONCA 901
**CanLII:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/13478/index.do

FACTS: The appellants, trustees of the Musicians' Pension Fund of Canada, purchased shares in Kinross Gold Corporation and commenced a putative class action in 2012 on behalf of persons who acquired Kinross shares between February 16, 2011 and January 16, 2012, claiming $4 billion in damages for alleged misrepresentations (para 1). The claims arose from Kinross's September 2010 acquisition of Red Back Mining Inc. for $8.72 billion, of which $5.54 billion was allocated to goodwill—$4.62 billion relating to the Tasiast mine in Mauritania (para 9). The appellants alleged that Kinross overstated goodwill by failing to record a timely impairment charge for the Tasiast mine despite adverse 2011 drilling results (the "Goodwill Impairment Claim"), and wrongly represented that the Tasiast expansion project remained on schedule (the "Expansion Claim") (paras 2-3). Kinross ultimately recorded a $2.94 billion goodwill impairment charge in early 2012 (para 24). The motion judge dismissed the leave and certification motion, finding the expert accounting evidence supporting the Goodwill Impairment Claim fatally flawed and declining to consider the Expansion Claim because it was not pleaded (paras 5-6).

ISSUES: Whether the motion judge erred in finding there was no reasonable possibility that the Goodwill Impairment Claim would succeed at trial by misapprehending the leave test under s. 138.8(1) of the Securities Act, exceeding his proper role, assuming all relevant information was before the court, and mischaracterizing the claim (para 31). Whether the motion judge erred by failing to consider the Expansion Claim on the basis that it was not pleaded (para 31). Whether the motion judge erred by denying certification of the common law misrepresentation claims solely because he had denied leave to proceed with the statutory claims (para 32).

DECISION: The Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal from the motion judge's leave and certification rulings (para 8). The court held that the motion judge did not err in finding no reasonable possibility that the Goodwill Impairment Claim would succeed, properly declined to consider the unpleaded Expansion Claim, and correctly denied certification of the common law claims (paras 8, 31-32).

REASONS: The court affirmed that under s. 138.8(1)(b) of the Securities Act, leave requires a "reasonable possibility" that the action will be resolved in the plaintiff's favour at trial, and that this standard is the "same test" used for determining whether a claim has a reasonable prospect of success for certification purposes under s. 5(1)(a) of the CPA (paras 42-45). However, the court emphasized that while the standard is identical, it is "applied in entirely different contexts"—on a leave motion, the standard is applied to a paper record of affidavit evidence and cross-examinations, whereas on certification the facts pleaded are taken as true (paras 43-45). The court found the motion judge correctly understood that the leave test is a "genuine screening mechanism" requiring the court to "assess and weigh the evidence" (para 36). The court rejected the appellants' argument that the motion judge misapprehended the test by expressing it in negative terms, finding no error in his formulation (paras 38-39). The court also upheld the motion judge's refusal to consider the Expansion Claim because it was not pleaded, and his conclusion that denial of leave on the statutory claims was dispositive of certification of the common law claims (paras 6, 31-32).

SIGNIFICANCE: This case clarifies the relationship between the leave test under s. 138.8(1)(b) of the Ontario Securities Act and the certification test under the Class Proceedings Act, confirming that while the "reasonable possibility" standard is the same, it operates in fundamentally different evidentiary contexts—a distinction critical to how courts assess statutory misrepresentation claims in securities class actions.

## 4. *Odhavji Estate v. Woodhouse*, 2003 SCC 69
**CanLII:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/2104/index.do

FACTS: On September 26, 1997, Manish Odhavji was fatally shot by Metropolitan Toronto Police officers while fleeing from his vehicle after a bank robbery (para 2). The Special Investigations Unit ("SIU"), a civilian agency mandated to investigate police conduct in cases of death or serious injury, commenced an investigation and requested that the involved officers remain segregated, attend same-day interviews, and provide shift notes, on-duty clothing, and blood samples (para 3). The officers allegedly failed to comply with these requests, did not attend interviews until September 30, and provided statements that were inaccurate and misleading (para 4). The Odhavji estate and family sued the officers, Chief of Police Boothby, the Police Services Board, and the Province, alleging misfeasance in public office and negligence, claiming the inadequate investigation caused them mental distress, anger, depression, and anxiety (paras 4-5).

ISSUES: Whether the tort of misfeasance in public office can arise from a public officer's failure to perform a statutory duty, or whether it is limited to unlawful exercises of statutory or prerogative powers (para 9). Whether the plaintiffs' claims for misfeasance in public office against the officers and the Chief, and claims for negligence against the Chief, the Board, and the Province, disclose a reasonable cause of action sufficient to survive a motion to strike under rule 21.01(1)(b) (paras 5, 14). Whether the Chief, Board, and Province owed the plaintiffs a private law duty of care to ensure police officers cooperated with the SIU investigation (para 5).

DECISION: The appeal was allowed in part and the cross-appeal dismissed (para 1). The actions for misfeasance in public office against the police officers and the Chief, and the action in negligence against the Chief, were permitted to proceed; the negligence actions against the Board and the Province were struck from the statement of claim (para 1). The Court held it was not plain and obvious that the misfeasance claims against the officers and Chief must fail, nor that the negligence claim against the Chief must fail, but found the negligence claims against the Board and Province could not succeed (paras 1, 55, 65).

REASONS: Applying the stringent test from Hunt v. Carey Canada Inc., the Court held that a claim should be struck only if it is "plain and obvious" that it is "certain to fail because it contains a radical defect," with facts taken as pleaded (para 15). On misfeasance, the Court held that the failure of a public officer to perform a statutory duty can constitute the tort, which is not limited to unlawful exercises of statutory or prerogative powers (paras 20-24). The tort requires "(1) deliberate, unlawful conduct in the exercise of public functions; and (2) awareness that the conduct is unlawful and likely to injure the plaintiff" (para 32). The Court found the statement of claim pleaded each constituent element, as the officers' failure to cooperate and the Chief's failure to ensure cooperation constituted unlawful breaches of statutory duties under the Police Services Act (paras 46-49). However, the allegation that the defendants "ought to have known" harm would result was struck because misfeasance is an intentional tort requiring subjective awareness (para 50). On negligence, the Court applied the Anns/Cooper duty of care test and found a prima facie duty owed by the Chief based on reasonable foreseeability and proximity, with no policy reasons negating the duty (paras 52-55). The Board and Province, however, were too far removed from day-to-day police conduct and had no statutory obligation to ensure officer cooperation with the SIU, negating proximity (paras 60-65).

SIGNIFICANCE: This case establishes that the tort of misfeasance in public office extends to deliberate failures to perform statutory duties, not merely unlawful exercises of power, and clarifies the required mental element of subjective awareness of likely harm. It also confirms that a chief of police may owe a private law duty of care to members of the public in the context of ensuring officer cooperation with SIU investigations, while boards and provincial governments generally do not.

## 5. *Powder Mountain Resorts Ltd. v. British Columbia*, 2001 BCCA 619
**CanLII:** https://www.bccourts.ca/jdb-txt/ca/01/06/2001bcca0619.htm

FACTS: In 1985, Powder Mountain Resorts Ltd. ("PMR"), through its principals Annie Evelyn Hartwick and Dianne Hartwick, responded to a provincial call for expressions of interest to develop a commercial alpine ski resort in the Powder Mountain area of the Callaghan Valley near Whistler, British Columbia. PMR was the only party to submit a response by the July 2, 1985 deadline, and the Hartwicks subsequently sought exclusive development rights, an option to purchase Crown land for $1.00, and a 15-year lease at nominal rent—terms inconsistent with the Province's Commercial Alpine Ski Policy (dated February 14, 1986), which required market-value land sales and lease rentals of 2% of gross lift revenues. Negotiations continued through 1986 and into 1987, but the Province never agreed to PMR's substantive terms, and the Cabinet ultimately rejected PMR's proposal on its merits. PMR and the Hartwicks sued the Province and Premier William N. Vander Zalm, alleging breach of Contract A, breach of a duty to negotiate fairly and in good faith, negligent misrepresentation, unlawful interference with economic interests, and abuse of public office; the trial judge (Tysoe J.) dismissed the action in Reasons dated August 24, 1999, and the plaintiffs appealed.

ISSUES: The appeal raised whether the trial judge erred in finding that PMR's proposal was rejected on its merits rather than for improper reasons, and whether he made palpable and overriding errors of fact regarding the February 6, 1987 meeting and the alleged "cease and desist" directive. The court also considered whether the trial judge applied the wrong legal test for the tort of abuse of public office, particularly in light of the House of Lords' expanded formulation in Three Rivers District Council v. Bank of England (No. 3), [2000] 3 All E.R. 1. Additionally, the court addressed whether the Province owed and breached a duty to negotiate fairly and in good faith, and whether the claims of negligent misrepresentation and unlawful interference with economic interests were properly dismissed.

DECISION: The appeal was dismissed (para 1; Lambert J.A. concurring at para 75). The court upheld the trial judge's findings that the defendants were not in breach of any duty owed to the plaintiffs, that the provincial Cabinet had rejected PMR's proposal "on its merits," and that the defendants had acted only in the best interests of the Province (para 4). The court also affirmed the dismissal of the abuse of public office claim, finding that the plaintiffs failed to establish the required elements of the tort (paras 7-8).

REASONS: The court held that the tort of abuse of public office requires proof that a public official either exercised power for the specific purpose of injuring the plaintiff (bad faith in the sense of improper or ulterior motive) or acted "unlawfully with a mind of reckless indifference to the illegality of his act" and to the probability of injury to the plaintiff (para 7, quoting Lord Steyn in Three Rivers at 8). The court emphasized that negligent excess of power—acting without knowledge of or subjective recklessness as to unlawfulness—is not actionable, as confirmed by the House of Lords in Three Rivers, the High Court of Australia in Northern Territory v. Mengel (1995) 185 C.L.R. 307, and the New Zealand Court of Appeal in Garrett v. Attorney General [1997] 2 N.Z.L.R. 332 (para 7). Because abuse of public office is an intentional tort carrying the "stench of dishonesty," the court reiterated that "clear proof commensurate with the seriousness of the wrong should be provided" (para 8, citing MacMillan Bloedel Ltd. v. Galiano Island Trust Committee (1995) 10 B.C.L.R. (3d) 121 at 181-2). The court found no palpable and overriding errors in the trial judge's credibility findings, noting that he directly addressed the conflicting testimony and his findings were supported by the evidence (para 10). The court also upheld the trial judge's conclusion that the Ministry was willing to be "flexible" with respect to process but never agreed to forego the substantive requirements of the Ski Policy concerning lease tenure, rental, and land price (para 20).

SIGNIFICANCE: This case confirms that the tort of abuse of public office in Canada requires proof of subjective bad faith or subjective recklessness—not mere negligence or objective recklessness—and emphasizes that courts must exercise caution to avoid straying into political decision-making or becoming arbiters of officials' personal thought processes (paras 2, 7). It establishes that clear and convincing proof is required for allegations of bad faith against public officials, and that the tort will not lie where a government actor merely makes a decision adverse to a plaintiff's interests without the requisite intent or knowledge of illegality.

1. Yadeta v. The Regional Municipality of Peel Police Services Board, 2023 ONSC 6387

FACTS: Banti Yadeta, a self-represented plaintiff, was arrested in March 2015 and charged with offences related to pimping a minor, child pornography, and uttering threats. He was held in custody until bail was granted, and the matter proceeded to trial in February-March 2016. Midway through trial, a resolution was reached whereby Yadeta entered into a peace bond and forfeited materials, after which the charges were withdrawn. In 2022, Yadeta commenced civil actions against Peel Police Services Board, the Crown, the Attorney General, and Maplehurst Correctional Complex.

ISSUES: The court considered two motions: first, whether leave should be granted under s. 17 of the Crown Liability and Proceedings Act ("CLPA") to permit claims of misfeasance in public office and bad faith against the Crown; and second, whether the defendants' motions to dismiss all of Yadeta's claims under Rule 21.01(1)(b) should be granted.

DECISION: The court denied leave under s. 17 of the CLPA and granted the defendants' motions to dismiss all claims without leave to amend (paras. 4-5, 121).

REASONS: The court held that bald allegations of bad faith and malice are insufficient — material facts supporting the essential elements of the torts must be pleaded (para. 41). Yadeta acknowledged he lacked material facts to support his claims and intended to use discovery to find them, which the court held constitutes an impermissible "fishing expedition" (para. 45). The court further found that the charges had not been resolved in Yadeta's favour, as the withdrawal was part of a negotiated resolution involving a peace bond and forfeiture order, which is fatal to any malicious prosecution claim (para. 59). The Peel Police Board was additionally protected by s. 5(9) of the Police Services Act for actions taken in good faith (para. 95).

SIGNIFICANCE: This case establishes that under s. 17 of the CLPA, plaintiffs must plead specific material facts supporting misfeasance or bad faith at the outset — discovery cannot be used to find the factual basis for the claim. It also clarifies that a negotiated resolution of criminal charges does not constitute a termination in the plaintiff's favour for purposes of malicious prosecution.

2. Phixaykoune v. Ontario, 2024 ONSC 3860

FACTS: The plaintiff was severely beaten by fellow inmates on April 8, 2020, while incarcerated at Maplehurst Correctional Centre, sustaining life-altering injuries. The assault lasted over 90 minutes before correctional staff intervened. The plaintiff commenced proceedings in September 2022 against the Crown and anonymous correctional officers, and sought leave to amend his pleading to identify the officers by name.

ISSUES: Whether leave should be granted under s. 17 of the CLPA to lift the stay of proceedings and permit the plaintiff to name individual correctional officers, specifically whether the officers' conduct constituted bad faith rather than mere negligence, and whether a breach of fiduciary duty could be pleaded against the officers personally.

DECISION: The court denied leave to add correctional officers as defendants on the basis of bad faith or breach of fiduciary duty, but the stay remained lifted with respect to claims against the Crown (paras. 10, 27).

REASONS: The court held that bad faith requires subjective intent — a conscious wrongdoing — and that negligence or poor judgment, however egregious, does not constitute bad faith (para. 20). The plaintiff himself admitted the officers did not intend to see him harmed (para. 21). The Investigation Report findings — that officers failed to conduct twice-hourly patrols — were found to constitute negligence at most, not recklessness sufficient to amount to bad faith (paras. 24-26). The court further held that correctional officers as Crown employees owe no personal fiduciary duty to inmates absent specific facts establishing such a duty (paras. 7-8).

SIGNIFICANCE: This case clarifies that the threshold for bad faith under s. 17 of the CLPA requires evidence of subjective intent to cause harm, and that recklessness or systemic failures short of conscious wrongdoing will not suffice. It also confirms that Crown employees are immune from personal liability for negligence and that claims must be brought against the Crown.

3. Botosh v. Ministry of the Attorney General, 2026 ONSC 1541

FACTS: Michelle Botosh was employed at the Ottawa Courthouse from 2007 until 2014, when she was deemed to have abandoned her position. In 2021, she discovered that MAG employees allegedly made fraudulent misrepresentations to her disability insurer Manulife during her long-term income protection ("LTIP") application, and were operationally negligent in handling court documents. She commenced proceedings in November 2023 and brought a motion for leave under s. 17 of the CLPA.

ISSUES: Whether leave should be granted under s. 17 of the CLPA to permit Botosh's claims of fraudulent misrepresentation and other bad faith torts against the Crown, specifically whether she had established a reasonable possibility of success on the fraudulent misrepresentation claim.

DECISION: Leave was denied for all claims except operational negligence, which the Crown consented to proceed (paras. 128-129). The style of cause was amended to substitute "His Majesty the King in Right of Ontario" for the Ministry of the Attorney General (para. 130).

REASONS: The court applied the five-element test for fraudulent misrepresentation and found Botosh failed on multiple elements (paras. 63-108). Critically, the alleged misrepresentations were made to Manulife, not to Botosh, so the Crown could not have intended Botosh to rely on them, and Botosh could not establish she suffered harm as a result — her LTIP was in fact approved, and only cancelled when she chose to pursue studies in the UK (paras. 91-107). The court further held that a Ministry is not a suable entity and the proper defendant is "His Majesty the King in Right of Ontario" (para. 125). Several allegations were also statute-barred by the two-year limitation period under the Limitations Act, 2002 (paras. 116-122).

SIGNIFICANCE: This case confirms that a Ministry is not a suable entity under the CLPA and clarifies the elements of fraudulent misrepresentation in the Crown liability context. It also illustrates how courts apply the Theratechnologies "reasoned consideration" standard under s. 17 to screen claims at the leave stage.
