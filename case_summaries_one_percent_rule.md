## 1. *Fraser v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2020 SCC 28 (2020)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/18510/index.do

FACTS: The appellants are three retired RCMP members who took maternity leave in the 1990s and later enrolled in the RCMP’s job-sharing program, which allowed full-time members to split one position. Under the *Royal Canadian Mounted Police Superannuation Act* and Regulations, job-sharers were classified as part-time workers and could not buy back full-time pension credits for their reduced-hours service, unlike members on leave without pay. The appellants claimed this pension limitation constituted adverse-impact discrimination against women, as most job-sharers were women with children (paras 1-3, 10-12).

ISSUES: (1) Whether the pension plan’s denial of full-time pension credit to job-sharers creates a distinction based on sex and imposes a burden that reinforces, perpetuates, or exacerbates women’s disadvantage under s. 15(1) of the *Charter*. (2) If so, whether the infringement is justified under s. 1 (paras 4, 54-56).

DECISION: The appeal was allowed. The Court declared that the pension plan’s limitation on job-sharers’ ability to buy back full-time pension credits breaches s. 15(1) of the *Charter* and is not justified under s. 1 (paras 138-139).

REASONS: The majority held that the pension plan’s distinction—denying full-time pension credit to job-sharers while granting it to those on leave without pay—had a disproportionate impact on women, as job-sharers were predominantly women with children, and this perpetuated historical disadvantage in pension design (paras 76-77, 111-113). The Court rejected the lower courts’ reliance on the claimants’ “choice” to job-share, noting that differential treatment can be discriminatory even if based on choice (paras 64-66). The majority further held that the government failed to identify a pressing and substantial objective for the differential treatment, rendering the breach unjustifiable under s. 1 (paras 128-137).

## 2. *Transport North American Express Inc. v. New Solutions Financial Corp.*, 2004 SCC 7 (2004)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/2118/index.do

FACTS: New Solutions Financial Corp. advanced $500,000 to Transport North American Express Inc. under a credit agreement requiring interest at 4% per month plus various fees and royalties, resulting in an effective annual interest rate of 90.9%, exceeding the 60% criminal rate under s. 347 of the *Criminal Code*. The application judge applied "notional severance" to reduce the rate to 60%, but the Court of Appeal struck out the interest clause entirely, leaving other charges at a 30.8% effective rate (paras 1-3, 15).

ISSUES: Whether "notional severance" is available as a remedy for contracts violating s. 347 of the *Criminal Code*, and if so, whether it was appropriately applied in this case (para 4).

DECISION: The appeal was allowed, restoring the application judge's decision to apply notional severance and reduce the interest rate to the maximum legal rate of 60% (para 7). The court held that notional severance is available as a matter of law in s. 347 cases (para 5).

REASONS: The court held that a spectrum of remedies exists for statutory illegality, ranging from voiding the entire contract for exploitive arrangements to severance for otherwise unobjectionable contracts (para 6). The preferred severance technique is one that cures the illegality while remaining as close as possible to the parties' intentions, and blue-pencil severance may fundamentally alter the bargain by creating an interest-free loan where parties clearly intended to charge interest (paras 6, 24-25). The court applied four factors—whether severance subverts s. 347's purpose, whether parties had an illegal purpose, their bargaining positions, and potential for unjust windfall—and found all favoured notional severance here, given the commercial context, independent legal advice, and absence of evidence of criminal intent (paras 27-31).

## 3. *Bank of America Canada v. Mutual Trust Co.*, 2002 SCC 43 (2002)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/1983/index.do

FACTS: Reemark Sterling I Ltd. planned a condominium project and entered into a Takeout Mortgage Commitment (TOC) with the respondent trust company, which would pay Reemark and receive mortgage payments at compound interest. Reemark also obtained a construction loan from the appellant bank at compound interest, and assigned its TOC rights to the appellant. When the respondent refused to advance funds under the TOC and a subsequent agreement, the appellant sold the project at a shortfall and sued for breach of contract, seeking compound pre- and post-judgment interest (paras 4-8).

ISSUES: The primary issue was whether the trial judge had jurisdiction to award compound pre-judgment and post-judgment interest on a damages award for breach of contract, and if so, whether such an award was correct. This required interpreting ss. 128-130 of the Ontario *Courts of Justice Act*, particularly whether ss. 128(4)(g) and 129(5) encompass a right to compound interest at common law or in equity (paras 20, 24).

DECISION: The Supreme Court of Canada allowed the appeal and restored the trial judgment, which had awarded compound pre- and post-judgment interest at the rate specified in the loan agreement (para 3). The Court held that the trial judge had jurisdiction to award such interest and was correct in doing so (para 20).

REASONS: The Court held that compound interest is a more precise measure of the time-value of money than simple interest, as it treats unpaid interest as unpaid principal, and is the norm in banking and financial systems (paras 23-24). The Court reasoned that ss. 128(4)(g) and 129(5) of the *Courts of Justice Act* provide statutory authority to award compound interest where it is "payable by a right other than under this section," which includes the court's common law power to award contract damages and its equitable jurisdiction (paras 31-32). The Court further reasoned that awarding simple interest on a loan that bore compound interest would fail to provide the plaintiff with the value it would have received had the contract been performed, and would incentivize breach; this analysis applies equally to pre- and post-judgment interest (paras 33-35). In this case, the compound interest award satisfied both expectation and restitution damages, and prevented the respondent from profiting from its breach (paras 36-37).

## 4. *Whiten v. Pilot Insurance Co.*, 2002 SCC 18 (2002)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/1956/index.do

FACTS: Daphne Whiten’s home was destroyed by fire in January 1994. Her insurer, Pilot Insurance, refused to pay her claim, alleging arson despite no evidence supporting that allegation, and cut off living-expense payments, forcing her into financial distress. The jury awarded her $345,000 in compensatory damages and $1 million in punitive damages; the Court of Appeal reduced the punitive award to $100,000.

ISSUES: (1) Whether the jury’s $1 million punitive damages award should be restored, or whether it was excessive and should be reduced. (2) Whether the trial judge’s jury charge on punitive damages was adequate. (3) Whether punitive damages are available in a breach of insurance contract claim.

DECISION: The appeal was allowed and the jury’s $1 million punitive damages award was restored; the insurer’s cross-appeal against any punitive damages was dismissed (paras 1, 4, 74). The Court held that the award, though high, was within rational limits and not excessive on the exceptional facts (para 4).

REASONS: The Court held that punitive damages are available in contract cases where there is an “actionable wrong” independent of the breach, and here the insurer breached its distinct duty of good faith dealing (paras 53-54, 59). The insurer’s conduct was exceptionally reprehensible: it forced the insured to risk her only asset and $320,000 in legal costs to collect a $345,000 claim, pursued a contrived arson defence for over two years, and exploited her vulnerability (paras 1, 3, 67-68). The Court also outlined the proper jury instructions on punitive damages, but found the trial judge’s skeletal charge, though not ideal, was not a ground for appeal (paras 36-43, 74). The award was proportionate given the need for denunciation, deterrence, and retribution, and the combined $665,000 at risk put the $1 million award in perspective (paras 4, 74).

## 5. *Dickason v. University of Alberta*, [1992] 2 SCR 1103 (1992)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/915/index.do

FACTS: Olive Dickason, a tenured full professor at the University of Alberta, was forced to retire at age 65 under a mandatory retirement clause in the collective agreement between the university and its academic staff. She filed a complaint with the Alberta Human Rights Commission alleging age discrimination under s. 7 of the Individual's Rights Protection Act, which permits discrimination if the employer proves it is "reasonable and justifiable in the circumstances" under s. 11.1. A board of inquiry ruled in her favour, the Court of Queen's Bench upheld that decision, but the Court of Appeal reversed.

ISSUES: (1) Whether the university's mandatory retirement policy at age 65 was "reasonable and justifiable" under s. 11.1 of the Individual's Rights Protection Act. (2) Whether the appellate court should defer to the board of inquiry's findings of fact. (3) Whether the Oakes test for limiting Charter rights under s. 1 applies to the s. 11.1 defence, and if so, with what modifications.

DECISION: The appeal was dismissed; the university proved that mandatory retirement was reasonable and justifiable under s. 11.1 (para 1). The Court of Appeal's decision was upheld, and the board of inquiry's reinstatement order was overturned (para 1).

REASONS: The Court held that while human rights legislation must be interpreted broadly and defences narrowly, the Oakes test applies to s. 11.1 but without deference to a private defendant, and with flexibility and regard to context (paras 12-14). The Court declined to defer to the board's findings because the Act provided for a broad standard of appellate review, and the appellate courts stood in the same position as the trial judge with the same record (paras 16-18). The Court found the university's objectives—preserving tenure, promoting academic renewal, facilitating planning, and ensuring retirement with dignity—were pressing and substantial, rationally connected to mandatory retirement, and minimally impairing, given the absence of obvious alternatives and the unique role of universities (paras 24-31). The collective agreement, freely negotiated by parties with relatively equal bargaining power, was also evidence of reasonableness (paras 22-23).

## 6. *Bell Canada v. Quebec (Commission de la Santé et de la Sécurité du Travail)*, [1988] 1 SCR 749 (1988)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/326/index.do

FACTS: A pregnant Bell Canada employee refused an alternative position offered by her employer and submitted a protective re-assignment certificate under Quebec’s *Act respecting occupational health and safety* (paras 2-5). Bell Canada, a federal undertaking, challenged the constitutional applicability of the Act’s provisions (ss. 33, 36, 37, 40-45) before the Superior Court, which granted evocation; the Court of Appeal affirmed (paras 5, 13-14).

ISSUES: (1) Whether the provincial occupational health and safety statute is constitutionally applicable to a federal undertaking like Bell Canada (para 2). (2) If applicable, whether the provincial provisions are inoperative due to conflict with federal legislation (para 6).

DECISION: The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal, answering the first constitutional question in the negative: the Act is not applicable to Bell Canada (para 1). The second question was not addressed as it was unnecessary (para 1).

REASONS: The Court held that the pith and substance of the Act is working conditions, labour relations, and management of undertakings, not merely health and safety (paras 23-25). Since these matters are within exclusive federal jurisdiction for federal undertakings under s. 91(29) and s. 92(10), provincial legislation cannot apply without encroaching on that jurisdiction (paras 26-28). The double aspect theory was rejected because both federal and provincial schemes pursue the same objective with identical techniques, leaving no distinct provincial aspect (paras 31-33). The Court also clarified that impairment is not required—affecting a vital part of the undertaking suffices (para 35).

## 7. *Bronfman Trust v. The Queen*, [1987] 1 SCR 32 (1987)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/191/index.do

FACTS: The Bronfman Trust borrowed funds from a bank to make discretionary capital allocations to its beneficiary in 1969 and 1970, rather than liquidating its low-yield investment portfolio (paras 1, 4-6). The Trust deducted the interest paid on these loans, claiming the borrowing preserved its income-earning assets. The Minister disallowed the deductions, and the Tax Review Board and Federal Court, Trial Division upheld that decision, but the Federal Court of Appeal reversed (paras 13-20).

ISSUES: Whether interest on borrowed money is deductible under s. 20(1)(c)(i) of the *Income Tax Act* only when the loan is used directly to produce income, or also when the loan indirectly preserves income-producing assets that might otherwise have been liquidated (para 2). A subordinate issue was whether the answer depends on the taxpayer's status as a corporation, trust, or natural person (para 2).

DECISION: The appeal was allowed, and the deduction was disallowed (para 1). The court held that the interest was not deductible because the borrowed funds were not used for the purpose of earning income (para 45).

REASONS: The court held that the statutory deduction requires tracing borrowed funds to an identifiable eligible use, and the taxpayer's purpose in using the funds is what matters (paras 23-25). The court rejected the argument that borrowing to preserve income-earning assets indirectly qualifies, stating that the text of the Act requires a direct use for earning income and that allowing indirect preservation would undermine the provision's restricted purpose (paras 31-33). The court further noted that the Trust's assets preserved yielded a return grossly below the interest costs, and that preventing capital losses cannot support a deduction limited to earning income (paras 38-40).

## 8. *The Queen v. Beauregard*, [1986] 2 SCR 56 (1986)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/157/index.do

FACTS: The respondent, a Quebec Superior Court judge appointed on July 24, 1975, challenged s. 29.1 of the *Judges Act*, which required judges appointed after February 17, 1975 to contribute 6.5–7% of their salary toward pensions, while earlier appointees contributed only 1.5% (paras 2-7). The Federal Court held the provision violated s. 100 of the *Constitution Act, 1867*, but rejected the equality claim under s. 1(b) of the *Canadian Bill of Rights* (paras 9-15).

ISSUES: (1) Whether s. 29.1 of the *Judges Act* violated s. 100 of the *Constitution Act, 1867*, which requires Parliament to "fix and provide" judges' salaries and pensions (para 17). (2) Whether s. 29.1 violated s. 1(b) of the *Canadian Bill of Rights* by discriminating between judges based on appointment date (para 17).

DECISION: The appeal was allowed; s. 29.1 was upheld as constitutional (para 1). The court held that the provision did not violate s. 100 of the *Constitution Act, 1867* (para 45) and, by majority, did not violate s. 1(b) of the *Canadian Bill of Rights* (para 45). Beetz and McIntyre JJ. dissented in part, finding s. 29.1(2) violated s. 1(b) (para 45).

REASONS: The court held that judicial independence requires financial security, but s. 29.1 did not interfere with this principle because it merely applied a standard, widely used contributory pension scheme, and judges must bear their fair share of national costs (paras 23-25). Parliament's power under s. 100 is not limited to 1867 pension models, and there was no improper or colourable purpose or discriminatory treatment of judges (paras 31-35). On the *Bill of Rights* claim, the majority held that drawing a line between incumbents and future appointees was not discriminatory, and the choice of the date of first reading was not arbitrary or capricious (paras 40-44).

## 9. *Montréal v. Arcade Amusements Inc.*, [1985] 1 SCR 368 (1985)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/6275/index.do

FACTS: The City of Montréal adopted By-law 5156 regulating amusement machines and halls, including a provision (s. 8) prohibiting persons under eighteen from using amusement machines or entering amusement halls, and a provision (para. D of s. 12) prohibiting persons under eighteen from being admitted to billiard halls. The respondents, operators of amusement establishments, challenged the by-law’s validity on grounds that it was prohibitory, vague, discriminatory, and an unconstitutional invasion of federal criminal law power. The Superior Court dismissed the petitions, but the Court of Appeal reversed and annulled the by-law in its entirety.

ISSUES: (1) Whether By-law 5156 was ultra vires as disguised prohibitory legislation rather than valid regulation under the City’s zoning and commerce powers; (2) whether the by-law was void for vagueness; (3) whether s. 8 and para. D of s. 12 were discriminatory and therefore invalid; and (4) whether the by-law trenched on federal criminal law jurisdiction.

DECISION: The appeal regarding Arcade Amusements Inc. was dismissed, and the appeal regarding Fountainhead Fun Centres Ltd. et al. was allowed in part. The by-law was held invalid only in part: s. 8 and para. D of s. 12 were declared ultra vires and annulled (paras 45, 47).

REASONS: The Court held that the by-law was not prohibitory in nature; although s. 7 limited amusement halls to a small area, it did not amount to a prohibition, and the by-law permitted free operation in authorized premises (paras 23-25). The by-law was not void for vagueness, as the term “young children” in s. 2 was sufficiently understandable, and any interpretive difficulty did not invalidate the by-law (paras 31-33). However, s. 8 and para. D of s. 12 were discriminatory because the City’s enabling powers did not authorize distinctions based on age, and these provisions were severable from the rest of the by-law (paras 40-43). Finally, the by-law did not invade federal criminal law power, as its purpose was to regulate local commerce, zoning, and youth protection, not to prohibit gaming on moral grounds (paras 50-52).

## 10. *Phoenix Assurance v. Bird Construction*, [1984] 2 SCR 199 (1984)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/4609/index.do

FACTS: Phoenix Assurance (PUK and its Canadian subsidiary PCDA) entered into a complex development arrangement with Ownix Developments to construct a head office building in Toronto. Ownix transferred land to PCDA, received a ground lease back, and then granted a credit lease to PUK, which was assigned to Canada Trust as mortgage security. Bird Construction performed construction work under a contract with Ownix, including completion work and leasehold improvements for third-party tenants, but was unpaid when Ownix became insolvent. Bird registered liens against the interests of Phoenix, Ownix, and Canada Trust.

ISSUES: (1) Whether the Phoenix companies were "owners" within the meaning of s. 1(1)(d) of The Mechanics' Lien Act, R.S.O. 1970, c. 267, given that they did not directly contract with Bird. (2) Whether Bird was entitled to a lien against Phoenix's interests for completion expenditures and third-party tenant leasehold improvements. (3) If liens were valid, what interest rate and periods applied for pre-judgment and post-judgment interest.

DECISION: The appeal and cross-appeal were dismissed (para 45). The Court upheld the Court of Appeal's decision that Phoenix was liable for the completion expenditures and PCDA leasehold improvements, but not for the third-party tenant improvements (para 45).

REASONS: The Court held that "owner" under the Act requires (a) an estate or interest in the land, (b) a request for the work, and (c) that the work be done on behalf of, with privity of, or for the direct benefit of the party (paras 23-25). Taking an overall view of the development arrangements, both PUK and PCDA factually requested the work, notwithstanding that Ownix made the formal request (paras 26-28). The Court reasoned that the statutory pattern does not permit different results based on whether an owner contracted directly or through intermediaries (para 31). However, for the third-party tenant improvements, the Court held that no privity existed between Bird and Phoenix, and the Act did not make all owners jointly liable for work authorized by a single owner; the tenants were owners of their leasehold interests, and Phoenix made no request regarding those interests (paras 38-41). Interest was to be calculated at the judgment rate as ordered by the Court of Appeal, not at contractual or commercial rates (paras 43-44).

## 11. *Labatt Breweries of Canada Ltd. v. Attorney General of Canada*, [1980] 1 SCR 914 (1979)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/5687/index.do

FACTS: Labatt Breweries marketed a beer labelled “Special Lite” containing 4% alcohol by volume, while the Food and Drug Regulations prescribed that “Light Beer” contain no more than 2.5% alcohol. Labatt sought a declaration that its product was not likely to be mistaken for “light beer” under the regulations, and challenged the constitutional validity of the relevant provisions of the Food and Drugs Act and regulations.

ISSUES: (1) Whether Labatt’s labelling of “Special Lite” beer violated s. 6 of the Food and Drugs Act by being likely to be mistaken for “light beer” as defined in the regulations. (2) Whether ss. 6 and 25(1)(c) of the Food and Drugs Act and regulations B.02.130 to B.02.135 were constitutionally valid, particularly under the federal trade and commerce power.

DECISION: The appeal was allowed with costs, and a declaration issued that regulations B.02.130 to B.02.135 were invalid and that ss. 6 and 25(1)(c) of the Food and Drugs Act were ultra vires Parliament insofar as they related to malt liquors (para 914). The court held that the impugned provisions could not be supported under the criminal law, trade and commerce, or peace, order and good government powers (paras 914-915).

REASONS: The court held that the detailed regulation of the brewing industry, including standards for beer production and labelling, was not a proper exercise of the federal criminal law power, as it was not directed at protecting health or preventing deception in a criminal sense (paras 914-915). The trade and commerce power could not support regulation of a single industry, as the provisions were not concerned with interprovincial trade but with a “legal recipe” for brewing (para 915). The court further reasoned that the matter did not constitute a national emergency or a new problem of national concern, and thus could not be justified under the peace, order and good government clause (para 915).

## 12. *Andrews v. Grand & Toy Alberta Ltd.*, [1978] 2 SCR 229 (1978)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/2587/index.do

FACTS: James Andrews, a 21-year-old apprentice carman, was rendered a quadriplegic in a traffic accident caused by the respondents. He suffered a functional transection of the spinal cord, leaving him paralyzed from the neck down with partial use of his right arm, and dependent on others for all daily activities. The trial judge awarded $1,022,477.48 in damages, but the Appellate Division reduced this to $516,544.48, prompting an appeal to the Supreme Court of Canada on the assessment of damages.

ISSUES: The primary legal issues were: (1) whether the Appellate Division erred in reducing the award for future care from a home-care standard ($4,135/month) to an institutional standard ($1,000/month); (2) the correct principles for calculating pecuniary losses, including future care costs, loss of earning capacity, capitalization rates, and contingencies; and (3) the appropriate upper limit for non-pecuniary damages in cases of catastrophic injury.

DECISION: The appeal was allowed. The Supreme Court set general damages at $740,000, comprising $564,000 for future care, $76,000 for loss of prospective earnings, and $100,000 for non-pecuniary loss. Together with special damages of $77,344, the total award was $817,344, of which Andrews received 75% ($613,008) due to the uncontested apportionment of liability (para 45).

REASONS: The Court held that the Appellate Division erred in substituting a lower standard of care without showing the trial judge applied a wrong principle or made a wholly erroneous estimate; home care was reasonable and the $4,135/month figure was justified (paras 23-25). The Court further reasoned that compensation for loss of earning capacity, not lost earnings, should be based on the pre-accident working life expectancy, not the shortened life expectancy, and that a 20% discount for contingencies was appropriate (paras 31-33). The Court adopted a 7% capitalization rate to account for inflation and investment returns, and set $100,000 as the upper limit for non-pecuniary damages in catastrophic injury cases, absent exceptional circumstances (paras 40-42).

## 13. *Assessor for Port Alberni v. MacMillan Bloedel et al.*, [1974] SCR 83 (1973)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/5169/index.do

FACTS: The Assessor for Port Alberni assessed business taxes on four owner-occupied industrial properties owned by MacMillan Bloedel under s. 427 of the Municipal Act, R.S.B.C. 1960, c. 255. The Assessor calculated "annual rental value" by capitalizing the actual value of land and improvements at 6% and 8% respectively, then adding the real property taxes payable on the properties (paras 1-3, 5). The taxpayers appealed, arguing that real property taxes should not be included in the annual rental value.

ISSUES: The sole issue on appeal was whether, in ascertaining the "annual rental value" of real property for business tax assessment under s. 427 of the Municipal Act, the Assessor should include real property taxes paid on the property (para 1). The court also considered whether "annual rental value" should be interpreted from the perspective of the landlord's net return or the gross amount a tenant would pay.

DECISION: The appeal was dismissed, with Judson J. dissenting (para 45). The court held that the Assessor erred in adding real property taxes to the capitalized return when determining annual rental value (para 44).

REASONS: The majority, per Spence J., held that "value" is a word appropriate to the position of the owner, meaning the worth of the property to the owner, and that "annual rental value" must be considered from the landlord's viewpoint (paras 31-32). The court reasoned that value is what remains after deductions from gross receipts, as it represents the income providing a return on the landlord's investment; therefore, property taxes, which are not part of the return on investment, must be excluded (paras 32-33). The majority relied on English authorities, including Dobbs v. Grand Junction Waterworks Co. and Sheffield Waterworks Co. v. Bennett, which established that "annual value" means net value, not gross value (paras 34-40). Judson J., dissenting, would have held that annual rental value means the amount a willing landlord would accept and a willing tenant would pay, including real property taxes (paras 46-48).

## 14. *H.A. Roberts Limited v. Minister of National Revenue*, [1969] SCR 719 (1969)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/5029/index.do

FACTS: The appellant company carried on a real estate business and, as one of five separate departments, operated a mortgage business, with most revenue derived from agency contracts with Crown Life Insurance Company and Burrard Mortgage Investments Limited. In 1963, these agency contracts were terminated, and the appellant received $83,633.72 in compensation, closed its mortgage department, and treated the payments as capital receipts. The Minister assessed the amounts as income, and the Exchequer Court upheld that assessment.

ISSUES: The primary issue was whether the compensation received for the termination of the mortgage agency contracts constituted capital receipts or income for tax purposes. A subsidiary issue was whether the mortgage business constituted a separate business from the appellant's other activities, and if not, whether the loss of the agency contracts represented the loss of capital assets of an enduring nature.

DECISION: The appeal was allowed, and the assessment was set aside (para 45). The Supreme Court held that the compensation received was a capital receipt and not taxable income.

REASONS: The Court held that, applying the decision in *Frankel Corporation v. M.N.R.*, the mortgage department was a separate business, and the payments were compensation for the termination of that separate business, making them capital receipts (paras 23-25). The Court further reasoned that even if the mortgage business was not a separate business, the cancellation of the agency contracts represented the loss of capital assets of an enduring nature, the value of which had been built up over the years, and therefore the payments were capital receipts (para 31). The Court distinguished cases where compensation was treated as income, noting that here the loss destroyed the entire structure of the appellant's profit-making apparatus, not merely a single contract in the ordinary course of business (paras 33-35).

## 15. *Kelly Douglas & Company Limited v. Mutual Leaseholds Limited*, [1969] SCR 952 (1969)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/5047/index.do

FACTS: The appellant leased premises from the respondent, with the lease stipulating annual rent of $12,000 plus additional rent equal to 1% of annual gross sales exceeding that minimum. "Gross sales" was defined as revenues less certain deductions, including "any amount paid by the lessee hereunder for taxes and insurance premiums." The appellant sought rectification, claiming the true agreement was that taxes and insurance premiums should be deducted from the 1% of gross sales figure, not from gross sales themselves.

ISSUES: Whether the written lease should be rectified to reflect the appellant's alleged prior agreement that taxes and insurance premiums would be deducted from the percentage rent calculation rather than from gross sales. The central issue was whether the appellant had adduced sufficient evidence of a prior concluded agreement in the terms alleged.

DECISION: The Supreme Court of Canada dismissed the appeal with costs, affirming the concurrent findings of the trial judge and the Court of Appeal (para 1).

REASONS: The Court, speaking orally through the Chief Justice, held that the appellant's counsel had presented all possible arguments but the Court could not reverse the concurrent findings of fact made in the courts below (para 1). The Court found no basis to interfere with the lower courts' conclusion that the appellant had failed to prove a prior concluded agreement warranting rectification (para 1).

## 16. *Frankel Corporation Ltd. v. Minister of National Revenue*, [1959] SCR 713 (1959)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/7555/index.do

FACTS: The appellant company carried on four separate business operations, including a non-ferrous smelting and refining operation. In 1952, it sold that non-ferrous operation, including its inventory, to a purchaser, receiving $822,611.15 for inventory carried on its books at $744,515.47. The Minister treated the $78,095.68 difference as taxable profit from a sale of trading assets, but the Income Tax Appeal Board allowed the taxpayer's appeal; the Exchequer Court reversed, and the taxpayer appealed to the Supreme Court of Canada.

ISSUES: (1) Whether the sale of the non-ferrous inventory was a sale in the course of the taxpayer's business, making the profit taxable, or whether it was part of a sale of a separate business, making the profit non-taxable. (2) Whether, if the non-ferrous business was a separate business sold, the inventory must be treated as removed from stock-in-trade at market value, thereby creating a taxable profit.

DECISION: The appeal was allowed and the assessment was set aside; the amount of $78,095.68 was not taxable (paras 45-46). The court held that the sale of the inventory was part of a sale of a business, not a sale in the business, and the profit was not income from a business under s. 4 of the Income Tax Act.

REASONS: The court applied *Doughty v. Commissioner of Taxes*, [1927] A.C. 327, holding that the sale of the inventory was not a sale in the taxpayer's business but was made as part of a sale of a business, and therefore the proceeds were not income from a business (paras 40-42). The court rejected the Minister's argument that the taxpayer operated only one business, finding that the non-ferrous operation was a separate business based on its distinct equipment, staff, customers, and accounting (paras 33-38). The court also dismissed the *Sharkey v. Wernher* argument, holding that the inventory was not removed or diverted from stock-in-trade before sale; rather, the consideration for the inventory was received as part of the consideration for the whole transaction (paras 43-44).

## 17. *Laane & Baltser v. Estonian S.S. Line*, [1949] SCR 530 (1949)

## 18. *Barnes v. Saskatchewan Co-operative Wheat Producers Ltd. et al.*, [1947] SCR 241 (1947)

The association issued certificates in 1929 promising interest on these deductions, but reserved the right to reduce or eliminate interest in any year. After financial difficulties, the association ceased paying interest in 1931 and later paid patronage dividends to current growers, while the appellant, who had retired from farming, sought repayment of his deductions and interest.

ISSUES: (1) Whether the appellant was entitled to repayment of the commercial reserves and elevator deductions, or to interest thereon, based on the contracts or certificates. (2) Whether the association breached a trust or contractual obligation by failing to pay interest before distributing patronage dividends to later shareholders. (3) Whether the appellant was entitled to a declaratory judgment defining his rights.

REASONS: The contracts contained no covenant to repay the deductions or to pay interest, and the association used the funds within the terms of the contracts, so there was no breach of covenant or trust (paras 23-25). The certificates' proviso allowing the association to reduce or eliminate interest was not repugnant but qualified the covenant, and the association's resolutions were mere expressions of intention (paras 31-33). ## 19. *Gatineau Power Co. v. Crown Life Insurance Co.*, [1945] SCR 655 (1945)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/8347/index.do

FACTS: The appellant company issued bonds payable in either Canadian or US funds at the holder's option, redeemable before maturity. The company elected to redeem the bonds on June 1, 1939, when the exchange premium on US funds was 13/64ths of 1%. The respondent holder did not present the bonds until September 20, 1939, when the premium had risen to 11%, and the company refused to pay the face value in US funds at that rate.

ISSUES: (1) Whether the holder's option to receive payment in US funds was limited to the exact redemption date or continued until actual payment. (2) If the option continued, what date should be used to convert the US dollar obligation into Canadian currency for the purpose of judgment.

DECISION: The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal and affirmed the appellate court's judgment, ordering the appellant to pay in Canadian funds an amount sufficient to purchase the required US funds at the exchange rate current on September 20, 1939 (para 655).

REASONS: The Court held that the privilege of receiving payment in two currencies was not limited to the day of maturity, as no such express limitation existed in the bond clause and implying one would contradict the plain meaning of the language (para 655). The Court further reasoned that the company's obligation was to maintain readiness to pay until the debt was discharged, and the holder had no duty to present the bonds on any particular day; therefore, the breach occurred on September 20, 1939, when payment was refused, and conversion into Canadian funds was to be made as of that date (para 655).

## 20. *The King v. Montreal Telegraph Co.*, [1945] SCR 669 (1945)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/8350/index.do

FACTS: The Crown, in right of Quebec, claimed $38,375.85 in taxes from the Montreal Telegraph Company (MTC) under the Corporation Tax Act, which imposed an annual $1,000 tax on "every telegraph company and every other company working a telegraph system for the use of the public." MTC had entered into an 1881 agreement with the Great North Western Telegraph Company (GNWTC), whereby GNWTC agreed to work, manage, and operate MTC's telegraph system for 97 years in exchange for a fixed annual remuneration, and GNWTC agreed to pay all taxes and assessments on MTC's property. MTC brought an action in warranty against GNWTC seeking indemnification if the Crown's tax claim succeeded.

ISSUES: (1) Whether MTC was liable for the tax imposed by the Corporation Tax Act, either as a "telegraph company" working a telegraph system or as an ordinary "incorporated company carrying on any undertaking, trade or business." (2) Whether MTC's action in warranty against GNWTC could succeed given the dismissal of the principal action, and what costs should be awarded between MTC and GNWTC.

DECISION: The Supreme Court dismissed the Crown's appeal in the principal action, affirming that MTC was not liable for the tax (para 45). The Court also dismissed MTC's appeal in the action in warranty, but modified the costs order so that GNWTC would not recover its costs against MTC (para 46).

REASONS: The Court held that the statute required a company to be "doing business in the province" and "working a telegraph system for the use of the public," and MTC did not meet this description because the 1881 agreement placed GNWTC in MTC's shoes, making GNWTC the operator for its own account, not as MTC's agent (paras 23-25). The Court further reasoned that MTC could not be taxed under the general clause for ordinary incorporated companies because the claim was specifically for the telegraph company tax, and MTC was not carrying on any undertaking, trade, or business (paras 31-33). Regarding the action in warranty, the Court followed *Archbald v. de Lisle* (25 Can. S.C.R. 1), holding that since the principal action was dismissed, the warranty action had no legal basis, but given the circumstances, GNWTC should not recover its costs against MTC (paras 40-44).

## 21. *Wilson v. The King*, [1938] SCR 317 (1938)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/8572/index.do

FACTS: Effie Wilson, widow and executrix of George S. Wilson, sought to recover $10,000 paid by her late husband to the Crown for a government life annuity. At the time of purchase, Wilson was 73, in poor health, and of unsound mind, acting on fixed delusions against his wife and son. The local postmaster, Schooley, through whom the payment was made, knew of Wilson's condition, but the administering Crown officers did not. Wilson received seven monthly annuity payments ($882.49) before dying.

ISSUES: (1) Whether the Crown could be held liable for a contract entered into with a person of unsound mind where the contracting officers lacked knowledge of the incapacity. (2) Whether the local postmaster's knowledge of Wilson's condition could be imputed to the Crown. (3) Whether restitution was barred because the contract had been fully executed on both sides.

DECISION: The appeal was allowed; the suppliant was entitled to recover $9,117.51 (the $10,000 less annuity payments) with interest from the date of the petition of right, with costs throughout (paras 45-46). Kerwin J. dissented.

REASONS: The Chief Justice held that the contract was improvident and made under mental incapacity, and that a court of equity would set it aside if made with a private party having knowledge of the facts (paras 23-25). The postmaster was an agent of the Crown for receiving annuity payments under the Government Annuities Act and regulations, and his knowledge of Wilson's condition was imputed to the Crown; it was his duty to communicate such facts to his superiors (paras 31-35). The fact that the contract was executed did not bar relief, as complete restitution could be made regarding the money actually paid, and the benefit of the annuity's chances was illusory given Wilson's health (paras 38-42). Davis J. concurred, adding that even without imputing the postmaster's knowledge, the court could grant relief given the manifest injustice of the bargain (paras 47-50).

## 22. *Reference Re Alberta Statutes - The Bank Taxation Act; The Credit of Alberta Regulation Act; and the Accurate News and Information Act*, [1938] SCR 100 (1938)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/2777/index.do

FACTS: The Governor General in Council referred three bills passed by the Alberta Legislative Assembly in 1937 to the Supreme Court of Canada for a constitutional opinion: the Bank Taxation Act (imposing a prohibitive tax on banks), the Credit of Alberta Regulation Act (establishing a provincial credit system), and the Accurate News and Information Act (compelling newspapers to publish government responses to criticism). These bills were part of a broader "Social Credit" legislative scheme aimed at reforming the province's monetary and credit system, and were reserved for federal consideration.

ISSUES: The court considered whether each of the three bills fell within the legislative competence of the province under section 92 of the British North America Act, 1867, or whether they invaded exclusive federal powers under section 91, particularly over banking, currency, trade and commerce, and criminal law. A threshold issue was whether the underlying Alberta Social Credit Act itself was constitutionally valid, as the three bills were ancillary to that scheme.

DECISION: The court held that all three bills were ultra vires the Alberta legislature. The Bank Taxation Act was invalid as an intrusion on federal jurisdiction over banking (para 45). The Credit of Alberta Regulation Act was invalid as legislation in relation to banking and trade and commerce (para 52). The Accurate News and Information Act was invalid as part of the unconstitutional Social Credit scheme and as an infringement on federal criminal law power over freedom of the press (paras 60-62). The court also held that the Alberta Social Credit Act itself was ultra vires (para 40).

REASONS: The court reasoned that the Social Credit Act, which established a provincial credit commission and monetary machinery, was not within provincial powers over property and civil rights (s. 92(13)) or matters of a local nature (s. 92(16)), but rather fell within federal jurisdiction over "Currency" (s. 91(14)) and "Banks and Banking" (s. 91(15)) (paras 23-25). The Bank Taxation Act, though framed as a tax, was designed to be prohibitive and to force federally-chartered banks out of the province, thus constituting an invalid interference with the federal banking system (paras 45-47). The Credit of Alberta Regulation Act was ancillary to the invalid Social Credit scheme and also regulated banking and trade (para 52). Regarding the press bill, the court held that provincial legislatures cannot abrogate the right of public discussion, which is protected by the federal Parliament's criminal law power, and that the Act was an attempt to amend the Criminal Code (paras 60-62).

## 23. *Attorney General of Canada v. Halifax / Fairbanks v. City of Halifax*, [1926] SCR 349 (1926)
**URL:** https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/9156/index.do

FACTS: The estate of James P. Fairbanks owned premises in Halifax leased to His Majesty the King for use as a Canadian National Railways ticket office. The lease required the lessee to pay "business taxes, if any," while the lessors paid other taxes. The City of Halifax assessed the estate for a business tax under s. 394 of the city charter, which deemed property let to the Crown to be in the occupation of the owner for tax purposes.

ISSUES: (1) Whether s. 394 of the Halifax city charter, which made the owner liable for business tax where the Crown tenant was exempt, was intra vires the provincial legislature under s. 92(2) of the B.N.A. Act. (2) Whether the tax imposed on the owner was an indirect tax, and therefore beyond provincial powers, or a permissible direct tax.

DECISION: The appeal was allowed, and the assessment against the Fairbanks estate was set aside. The court held that s. 394 was ultra vires as imposing an indirect tax (paras 45-46).

REASONS: The court held that a tax is indirect when imposed on a person in contemplation that another will pay it, and this intention may be inferred from the form of the tax or its general tendencies (paras 23-25). Here, the legislature could not have expected the landlord to bear the tax; rather, the landlord was substituted for the exempt Crown tenant, and the tax would naturally be passed on to the tenant as part of the rent, given the ordinary course of business and the tax's basis on the value of the premises occupied (paras 31-33). The court distinguished *City of Montreal v. Attorney-General for Canada*, [1923] A.C. 136, as that case involved a tax on the occupant's beneficial interest, not a tax shifted to an exempt tenant (para 38). Duff J. dissented, arguing that the incidence of local rates is too complex to presume shifting, and the legislation should be presumed valid unless affirmatively shown to be indirect (paras 50-52).

After the assignee successfully recovered the insurance moneys through litigation funded by all creditors, the appellant sought to claim part of those moneys as a secured creditor.

The court further reasoned that permitting the appellant to claim now would be inequitable to other creditors, as the appellant had participated in and benefited from the assignee's litigation at common expense, and its conduct amounted to abandonment or estoppel (paras 5-6, 31-33). ## 27. *City of Halifax v. The McLaughlin Carriage Co.*, (1907) 39 SCR 174 (1907)

## 29. *Biggs v. Freehold Loan and Savings Co.*, (1901) 31 SCR 136 (1901)

## 31. *Liverpool and London and Globe Ins. Co. v. Wyld and Darling*, (1877) 1 SCR 604 (1877)

The agent inspected the premises, accepted an increased premium, and issued an interim receipt, but the subsequent policy described only the original premises (S.T. 272). After a fire, the insurer refused to pay for goods lost in the added flats, and the plaintiffs sought to reform the policy.

ISSUES: Whether the contract of insurance, as evidenced by the application, written notice, and interim receipt, covered goods in the added flats, notwithstanding the policy’s narrower description. Whether the plaintiffs were entitled to equitable reformation of the policy after a loss, and whether the insurer’s agent had authority to bind the company to such coverage.

The appeal was dismissed, but costs of the appeal were refused due to an equal division of opinion among the judges (para 3).

REASONS: The majority held that the application, written notice, and interim receipt, read together, established a binding contract of insurance covering the goods in the added flats, and that the subsequent policy did not supersede this prior agreement (paras 1-2). The court reasoned that the agent’s knowledge and acceptance of the additional risk, coupled with the premium paid, bound the insurer, and equity would reform the policy to conform to the actual contract (paras 1-2).
