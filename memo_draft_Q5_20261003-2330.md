## Q5. Recent Appellate and Frequently Cited Mandamus Decisions

The last decade of appellate authority on mandamus has not disturbed the framework established by the Federal Court of Appeal in *Apotex Inc. v. Canada (Attorney General)*, [1994] 1 F.C. 742, affirmed [1994] 3 S.C.R. 1100; what the recent decisions do is harden the framework's gatekeeping function. The Supreme Court of Canada's contribution is *Law Society of Saskatchewan v. Abrametz*, 2022 SCC 29. In reasons for judgment by Rowe J. (Côté J. dissenting), the Court stated that "[m]andamus can be sought to compel administrative decision makers to carry out their duties and, in so doing, to limit delay in administrative proceedings" (*Abrametz*, 2022 SCC 29 at para 80, citing *Blencoe* at para 150), and added that "[m]y comments here do not intend to change any of the standards applicable for obtaining an order of mandamus generally. They merely affirm that it may also be an appropriate tool to prevent and address abuse of process" (at para 81). Abrametz therefore confirms the availability of mandamus in the delay context without altering the substantive test.

### The *Apotex* prerequisites as restated on appeal

The eight-part *Apotex* test remains the governing test in the Federal Courts. In *Wasylynuk v. Canada (Royal Mounted Police)*, 2020 FC 962 at para 73, the Court records that "[t]he criteria for an order of mandamus are found in the reasons of Robertson JA in *Apotex Inc. v Canada (Attorney General)*, [1994] 1 FC 742 (CA), at para 55, aff'd [1994] 3 SCR 1100", and that "[t]hese criteria have been confirmed recently by the Federal Court of Appeal in *Hong v Canada (Attorney General)*, 2019 FCA 241 (Woods, JA), at para 10"; the same paragraph notes that the criteria have been confirmed by the Federal Court of Appeal in *Canada (Health) v. The Winning Combination Inc.*, 2017 FCA 101 and *Lukács v. Canada (Transportation Agency)*, 2016 FCA 202, and applied by the Federal Court in *Express Gold Refining Ltd. v. Canada (National Revenue)*, 2020 FC 614, *Canadian Horse Defence Coalition v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2019 FC 1559 and *Albatal v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration)*, 2016 FC 371. At para 74, *Wasylynuk* adds that "[t]he *Apotex* criteria have also been applied recently and regularly by this Court, including in *Iris Technologies v Minister of National Revenue*, 2020 FC 532, at para 53 (Heneghan, J), aff'd 2020 FCA 117 (Rennie, JA)." *Ghaddar v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration)*, 2023 FC 946 at para 20 states that "[t]hese criteria have been confirmed by the Federal Court of Appeal in *Hong v Canada (Attorney General)*, 2019 FCA 241 at paragraph 10". *Mamut v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration)*, 2024 FC 1593 at para 86 summarizes the requirements: "(1) there must be a public legal duty to act; (2) the duty must be owed to the applicant; (3) there must be a clear right to performance of that duty". *Professional Association of Foreign Service Officers v. Canada (Treasury Board)*, 2026 FC 1136 at para 57 similarly states that the Federal Court of Appeal "identified eight preconditions that must be met for an applicant to be entitled to an order of mandamus", and at para 58 records that "[i]n *Benison* FCA, Heckman JA recently clarified the burdens that arise at each step of the mandamus analysis in the Federal Courts"; that decision further notes that leave to appeal to the Supreme Court of Canada was granted in *Benison* as of July 23, 2026 (at para 205). *Canadian Association of Elizabeth Fry Societies v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2026 FC 958 at para 203 states the conjunctive nature of the test — "[t]he test is conjunctive: if any of the eight criteria are not favourable to the Applicant, mandamus will be denied: *Benison* at para 45" — and at para 211 that, the first four criteria having been made out, the applicant "has a prima facie entitlement to mandamus: *Benison* at para 50."

### The public legal duty gate

The recent appellate authority is most consistent on the first prerequisite. In *Canada (Citizenship and Immigration) v. Nilam*, 2017 FCA 44 at para 27, the Court concluded that "the Minister does not have a public legal duty to continue processing the respondent's application notwithstanding that the RPD cessation proceedings have yet to be determined", with the consequence that mandamus was unavailable. *Canada (Public Safety and Emergency Preparedness) v. LeBon*, 2013 FCA 55 confirms the same requirement at a general level, noting at para 13 that "this Court noted that this line of cases is part of the general law of mandamus and invited the parties to make wider submissions at the hearing concerning the availability of mandamus in these circumstances." In *Chad v. Canada (National Revenue)*, 2025 FCA 102 at para 15, the Court states that "[f]or relief in the nature of mandamus to be granted, there must exist a public legal duty to act: *Apotex Inc. v. Canada (Attorney General)*, [1994] 1 FC 742 (FCA) at 766, aff'd [1994] 3 SCR 1100"; the same decision distinguishes the trial-level *Thornton* decision on solicitor-client privilege (at para 23). *O'Driscoll v. Canada (Royal Canadian Mounted Police)*, 2025 FCA 206 holds that "the first criterion for mandamus, namely whether there is a public legal duty, is assessed on a correctness basis" (at para 10); that "[t]hat test requires the appellant establish, inter alia, that the Commissioner owed her a public legal duty to act, and that she has a clear right to the Commissioner's performance of that duty" (at para 19); and that "a grant of statutory authority to a public official does not give rise to a legal right on the part of a citizen" (at para 22). The British Columbia Court of Appeal is to the same effect: in *Molberg v. British Columbia (Superintendent of Motor Vehicles)*, 2018 BCCA 12, the order under appeal was "[c]learly ... an order in the nature of mandamus purporting to compel the performance of a public legal duty (the very essence of mandamus)" (at para 23), but "[t]hat cannot be directed; the Superintendent is not under a public duty to take the action ordered; mandamus cannot lie: *Berg v. British Columbia*, 2006 BCCA 146 at para. 15" (at para 25), the Court relying on the trial-level decision in *Stenner v. British Columbia (Superintendent of Motor Vehicles)*, 2016 BCSC 1690 for the absence of jurisdiction to reopen a concluded hearing (at para 21).

### The two routes to a mandatory order, and the delay route

The Federal Court of Appeal's most compact statement of the modern test is *Doyle v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2022 FCA 56, where the reasons delivered from the bench state that "[m]andatory orders, or what the *Federal Courts Act*, R.S.C. 1985, c. F-7 calls mandamus, are not freely available. They are available in two narrow circumstances" (at para 5); "[f]irst, courts can grant mandatory orders or mandamus where the 'evidence can lead only to one result'" (at para 6); and "[s]econd, courts can grant mandatory orders or mandamus in circumstances of extreme maladministration. A very high threshold must be met before relief can be granted: see *D'Errico* and *LeBon*, above" (at para 7). Those two earlier authorities remain the source of the delay branch, notwithstanding that they predate a strict ten-year window on a 2026 reference date. In *D'Errico v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2014 FCA 95 at para 14, the Court characterized the relief sought as, "[i]n effect, ... the remedy of certiorari and mandamus"; at para 16 it stated that "[n]ormally, this Court awards mandamus only where the outcome of the case on the merits is a foregone conclusion – in other words the evidence can lead only to one result", and that "[o]ne recognized exception is where there has been substantial delay and the additional delay caused by remitting the matter to the administrative decision-maker for re-decision threatens to bring the administration of justice into disrepute", the word "exceptionally" recognizing "that administrative tribunals should be allowed another chance to decide the merits of the matter" (at para 17). *LeBon* supplies the parallel formulation on the merits: it was "open to the Federal Court to conclude on this evidence that the only lawful exercise of discretion is the granting of transfer" (at para 14), and "in the unusual circumstances of this case, mandamus is also available to prevent the further delay and harm that would be caused to Mr. LeBon if the Minister were given a third chance" (at para 14). At the provincial appellate level, *Morris v. British Columbia (Workers' Compensation Board)*, 2020 BCCA 293 states that "[t]he proper remedy for delay is an order for mandamus, compelling the public body to render a decision: *Wu v. Vancouver (City)*, 2019 BCCA 23" (at para 10), and that where a statutory decision maker has failed to discharge its responsibilities, "the worker's remedy is to ask the court to direct the decision maker to fulfill its duty" (at para 11). *Mamut*, 2024 FC 1593 at para 87 confirms the maladministration branch at first instance, stating that "[s]eparate and apart from the specific *Apotex* requirements, mandamus is also available as a remedy for 'extreme maladministration' on the part of administrative decision makers."

The weight of appellate authority runs against any expansive use of the delay route. *Garshowitz v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2017 FCA 251 holds that "[t]his is not a case where the outcome on the merits is certain enough to grant mandamus" and that "[n]or does the evidentiary record support mandamus because of severe maladministration" (at para 8), the Court adding that "[t]his is not a situation where I would cause any end result to be dictated to the General Division through a mandamus order" (at para 27). *Glacier Resorts Ltd. v. British Columbia (Minister of Environment)*, 2019 BCCA 289 contains the clearest remedial statement: "[i]f the Minister acts unreasonably by failing to consider evidence that must be considered, the proper remedy is to remit the matter for reconsideration" (at para 65), and the chambers judge "made no error in rejecting the estoppel, legitimate expectations, and mandamus arguments" (at para 66).

### The single-lawful-outcome route and the combination of certiorari and mandamus

The Federal Court of Appeal has recently described the combined remedy in *Canada (Citizenship and Immigration) v. Tennant*, 2019 FCA 206, where the majority reasons state that "[i]t is now well-established that this form of relief, a combination of certiorari and mandamus, is available where on the facts and the law there is only one lawful response, or one reasonable conclusion, open to the administrative decision-maker" (at para 72), and that the applicable language "can be understood to capture, among other things, a remedy of indirect substitution granted because there is only one reasonable outcome" (at para 74). Those passages are, however, properly characterized as obiter [verify]. The same one-outcome premise underlies the British Columbia Court of Appeal's grant in *Qterra Properties Ltd. v. Delta (Corporation)*, 2016 BCCA 504, where the Court proceeded on the agreed basis that "if the definition of retail trade in the LUC permits an LRS, the order for mandamus should be granted" (at para 14), and granted "an order in the nature of mandamus requiring the respondents to issue a business licence for an LRS to Tavolini" (at para 56). The limited scope of that reasoning should be noted: the sole question was the interpretation of a defined use.

### Mandatory orders in disguise, jurisdiction and deference

*Canada v. Boloh 1(a)*, 2023 FCA 120 is the leading recent appellate treatment of orders that compel executive action without being labelled mandamus. The Court held that the reviewing court must examine substance rather than form: "[i]nstead, the court must determine the essential character and real essence of the remedy being sought: *JP Morgan Asset Management (Canada) Inc. v. Canada (National Revenue)*, 2013 FCA 250, [2014] 2 F.C.R. 557 at paras. 49-50" (at para 61); and for the governing prerequisites it directs readers to "a statement of these prerequisites, see, e.g., *Canada (Public Safety and Emergency Preparedness) v. LeBon*, 2013 FCA 55, 444 N.R. 93 at para. 14" (at para 63). Two 2025 decisions round out the appellate picture. In *Canada (Prime Minister) v. Hameed*, 2025 FCA 118, the respondent "sought an order of mandamus or, in the alternative, a declaration pursuant to sections 18 and 18.1 of the *Federal Courts Act*" (at para 2), and the Court allowed the appeal and set aside the Federal Court's decision "for lack of jurisdiction" (at para 65); the disposition rests on jurisdiction rather than on the mandamus prerequisites. In *Chad*, 2025 FCA 102, in addition to the public-legal-duty holding at para 15, the Notice of Application was found to disclose no justiciable issue [verify] — a threshold distinct from the mandamus criteria and one that ought not be conflated with them.

### Contrary and qualifying appellate authority

Two appellate decisions qualify the framework in ways favourable to a party resisting mandamus. In *Cowichan Valley (Regional District) v. Wilson*, 2023 BCCA 25, the Court set aside the trial order of mandamus while expressly declining to decide the mandamus question: "it is unnecessary for me to consider whether it was unreasonable for the CVRD not to grant a height variance or whether mandamus was an appropriate remedy in this case" (at para 12); "[g]iven the disposition I propose, I would also set aside paragraph 1(c) of the order granting relief in the nature of mandamus" (at para 97). *Cowichan Valley* therefore contains no appellate analysis of the mandamus test, and it should not be cited for one. By contrast, the Ontario Court of Appeal in *wpd Sumac Ridge Wind Incorporated v. Kawartha Lakes (City)*, 2016 ONCA 496 held that an order requiring a municipality to reconsider an application in good faith is not mandamus: "[r]equiring the City to act in good faith is not mandamus, but rather a statement of an established principle of law" (at para 81). The City's submission that the *Apotex* test had to be applied before such an order could be made was advanced as party argument and rejected [verify]. Finally, *Zongshen (Canada) Environtech Ltd. v. Bowen Island Municipality*, 2017 BCCA 267 identifies the mandamus question on appeal — "whether Zongshen has satisfied the test for an order in the nature of mandamus compelling the Municipality to issue the building permit" (at para 2) — but the passages relied on describe the chambers judge's conclusion that the pre-tenure applications "were not valid and need not have been accepted, there being no basis for mandamus relief" (at para 18); the Court of Appeal's own disposition is not reproduced, so the decision is background only. *Zaghbib v. Canada (Public Safety and Emergency Preparedness)*, 2016 FCA 182, which traces "the seven criteria for the granting of an order of mandamus, as set out in the Federal Court decision in *Dragan v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration)*, 2003 FCT 211" (at para 15) and treats the discretionary mandamus decision as "reviewable on a deferential standard except where it is based upon an error of law" (at para 23), must be used with care: the Court itself characterized those substantive reasons as obiter and without precedential value [verify].

### Frequently cited trial-level decisions

The trial-level authorities most frequently relied upon cluster around two poles. On the test itself, the decisions most often cited are *Apotex* itself (in the "1993 CanLII 3004 (FCA)" formulation and the "[1994] 1 F.C. 742" formulation, adopted on the agreed statement of the parties in *Saravanabavanathan v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration)*, 2024 FC 564 at para 15), and *Dragan*, 2003 FCT 211, which is identified in *Zaghbib* at para 15 as the source of the seven-criteria formulation. On delay, the recurring reference is *Conille v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration)*, [1999] 2 F.C. 33 (T.D.) at para 23, cited in *Thomas v. Canada (Public Safety and Emergency Preparedness)*, 2020 FC 164 at para 19 and in *Saravanabavanathan*, 2024 FC 564 at para 16 ("[t]his test has been applied in the immigration context on many occasions"); *Thomas* also confirms the appellate source of the underlying criteria (at para 18). *Mamut*, 2024 FC 1593 collects the delay authorities on which the Federal Court now relies, including *Abdolkhaleghi v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration)*, 2005 FC 729 at para 26 — "This Court has held repeatedly that blanket statements like this are insufficient to justify delay" (at para 103) — together with *Kanthasamyiyar*, 2015 FC 1248, *Almuhtadi v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration)*, 2021 FC 712, *Jahantigh*, 2023 FC 1253, *Samideh*, 2023 FC 854, *Ran*, 2023 FC 1447 and *Saravanabavanathan*, 2024 FC 564, and states the evidentiary burden on the responding party: "[o]nce the third step of the *Conille* test has been reached, to avoid mandamus being ordered, the respondent must provide a reasoned explanation, supported by evidence, for the time that has been taken to make a decision" (at para 104). The same authorities appear in the granting decisions: *Bidgoly v. Canada (Citizenship and Immigration)*, 2022 FC 283 states that "[m]andamus compels the performance of a public legal duty that a public authority refuses or neglects to perform" and that "[u]nreasonable delay in performing the public duty may be deemed an implied refusal to perform" (at paras 14-15), and applies "*Almuhtadi*; *Bidgoly v Canada (Citizenship and Immigration)*, 2022 FC 283" in *Saravanabavanathan* at para 16. *Skibsted v. Canada (Environment and Climate Change)*, 2021 FC 416 confirms that "[a]n order for mandamus can compel the performance of a clear, affirmative legal duty by a public authority, but only when all of the *Apotex* criteria are met" (at para 127), and shows the test operating as a bar where the duty has not crystallized: "any further mandamus relief is premature because the Minister's duty to issue an action plan or protect critical habitat is only triggered after the final recovery plan is posted" (at para 57). *Professional Association of Foreign Service Officers*, 2026 FC 1136 illustrates the reverse: although the first four *Apotex* preconditions were met, relief was refused because a prospective order would have no practical value or effect. On the Canadian Charter/administrative side, *Canadian Association of Elizabeth Fry Societies*, 2026 FC 958 applied the test through the Federal Court of Appeal's decision in *Benison*, 2026 FCA 53 (at paras 203 and 211) [verify as to the citation of *Benison*], and *Canadian Association of Elizabeth Fry Societies* also relies on *Bedard*, 2024 FC 570, *Samideh*, 2023 FC 854 and *Bancroft*, 2020 NSSC 175 in setting out the surrounding framework [verify].

Taken as a whole, the weight of authority favours a restrictive approach: the Supreme Court in *Abrametz* leaves the general standards untouched (at para 81), and the Federal Court of Appeal — in *Nilam*, *Garshowitz*, *Doyle*, *Boloh*, *Chad* and *O'Driscoll* — has consistently insisted on a public legal duty and confined mandatory relief either to cases where the evidence admits of only one result or to extreme maladministration, with the British Columbia Court of Appeal taking the same restrictive view in *Molberg* and *Glacier Resorts*, and the Ontario Court of Appeal confining appellate intervention in *wpd Sumac Ridge*. The divergence lies not in the test but in its administration: the measurable volume of successful mandamus applications is at first instance in the Federal Court, particularly in immigration delay matters (*Thomas*, *Bidgoly*, *Ghaddar*, *Saravanabavanathan*, and *Mamut* in part), while the appellate courts continue to treat the remedy as exceptional. There is also a doctrinal point on which the decisions are not fully aligned: *Doyle* presents the maladministration branch as a second, self-standing narrow circumstance (at paras 5-7), whereas *Mamut* describes it as operating "[s]eparate and apart from the specific *Apotex* requirements" (at para 87) — a framing that may matter if the Supreme Court addresses the *Apotex* framework in *Benison*, where leave has been granted (as noted in *Professional Association of Foreign Service Officers*, 2026 FC 1136 at para 205).

### Gaps

No appellate decision in the past decade has re-examined the *Apotex* prerequisites at the Supreme Court of Canada; *Abrametz* is the only recent Supreme Court authority, and it expressly disclaims any change to the general standards (2022 SCC 29 at para 81). The most recent Federal Court of Appeal refinement of the burden at each step of the analysis is described in *Benison*, 2026 FCA 53, but it is available in the decisions under review only through the reasons of first-instance courts (*Canadian Association of Elizabeth Fry Societies*, 2026 FC 958 at paras 203 and 211; *Professional Association of Foreign Service Officers*, 2026 FC 1136 at paras 58 and 176), and leave to appeal to the Supreme Court has been granted (at para 205). The characterizations of *Zaghbib* and *Tennant* as obiter, and the Federal Court's reliance on *Almuhtadi*, *Kanthasamyiyar*, *Jahantigh*, *Ran*, *Samideh*, *Bedard* and *Bancroft* as frequently cited first-instance authority, rest on the descriptions given here and should be confirmed against the reports.
