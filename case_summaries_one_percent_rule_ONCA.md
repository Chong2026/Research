## 1. *Fram Elgin Mills 90 Inc. v. Romandale Farms Limited*, 2021 ONCA 201 (2021)
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/19511/index.do

FACTS: Romandale Farms Limited owned undeveloped lands in Markham and sold a 5% interest to Fram in 2003, with co-ownership agreements (COAs) restricting dispositions. In 2005, Romandale agreed to sell its remaining 95% interest to Kerbel, conditional on Fram’s consent or Romandale exercising buy-sell provisions. After litigation between Fram and Romandale, Fram and Kerbel entered a 2010 Settlement Agreement stating the sale of Romandale’s remaining interest would occur after secondary planning approval (SPA). Romandale later claimed this Settlement Agreement breached and repudiated the 2005 August Agreement, leading to consolidated actions.

ISSUES: The appeals raised whether the trial judge erred in finding (1) Romandale was not estopped from claiming the Settlement Agreement breached the 2005 August Agreement; (2) Kerbel breached the 2005 August Agreement by entering the Settlement Agreement; (3) Kerbel repudiated the agreement; (4) the agreement was frustrated or void for mistake; (5) Kerbel’s claim was limitation-barred; and (6) Kerbel was not entitled to specific performance.

DECISION: The Court of Appeal allowed the appeals, set aside the trial judgment, and ordered specific performance requiring Romandale to perform its obligations under the 2005 August Agreement (paras 17, 111-112). The court held the trial judge erred on all key issues, including estoppel by convention and repudiation.

REASONS: The court held that Romandale was estopped by convention from claiming the Settlement Agreement breached the 2005 August Agreement, as all parties shared and communicated the assumption that the sale would occur after SPA, and Fram and Kerbel relied on this to their detriment (paras 44-73). The court further held that Kerbel did not breach or repudiate the 2005 August Agreement by entering the Settlement Agreement, as the agreement did not alter Kerbel’s obligations and Romandale’s consent was not required (paras 74-107). The court also rejected Romandale’s arguments on frustration, mistake, and limitation periods, and found specific performance was the appropriate remedy given the unique nature of the lands (paras 103-107).

## 2. *Cobb v. Long Estate*, 2017 ONCA 717 (2017)
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/16103/index.do

FACTS: Wade Cobb was injured in a motor vehicle collision caused by Martin Long, who pleaded guilty to impaired driving; Long died before trial, and his estate became the defendant. The jury awarded Cobb $220,000 in compensatory damages, including $50,000 for past income loss, $100,000 for future income loss, and $15,000 for housekeeping losses. Before trial, Cobb had received $159,300 in statutory accident benefits (SABs) for income replacement and $9,150 for housekeeping benefits, including a $152,000 settlement allocated to income replacement, medical benefits, and other items (paras 4-10, 12).

ISSUES: The plaintiffs appealed, arguing that the trial judge erred in deducting the full SABs income replacement settlement from the combined past and future income loss awards, in refusing to put punitive damages to the jury, and in setting prejudgment interest. The defendant cross-appealed, arguing that the trial judge erred in failing to deduct all housekeeping benefits received, in applying the pre-August 2015 statutory deductible, and in awarding costs (paras 22-23).

DECISION: The Court of Appeal dismissed the plaintiffs’ appeal and allowed the defendant’s cross-appeal in part. The court upheld the deduction of SABs from income loss, the refusal to submit punitive damages to the jury, and the prejudgment interest rate, but held that the trial judge erred in failing to deduct the full housekeeping benefits and in applying the outdated statutory deductible; the costs award was remitted for reconsideration (paras 54-55, 63, 70, 76, 82, 87).

REASONS: The court held that s. 267.8(1) of the Insurance Act does not distinguish between past and future income loss, so the full $130,000 allocated to income replacement benefits in the SABs settlement was deductible from the combined jury award for income loss, preventing double recovery (paras 41-48). The court further held that the trial judge erred in not deducting the remaining $4,150 in housekeeping benefits from the future housekeeping award, as the statutory language requires deduction of all such benefits received before trial (paras 63-64). The court also held that the statutory deductible in force at the time of judgment ($36,540) should apply, as the amendment was procedural, not substantive (paras 70-71). The court found no error in refusing punitive damages because the evidence did not support a claim beyond compensatory damages (paras 55-56), and upheld the prejudgment interest rate as a proper exercise of discretion (paras 76-77). The costs award was remitted because the trial judge’s retroactivity analysis was flawed (paras 82-87).

## 3. *Sidhu v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Company*, 2014 ONCA 920 (2014)
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/13485/index.do

FACTS: The plaintiff, Harinder Kaur Sidhu, sued State Farm for statutory accident benefits following a car accident. During the jury trial, the defendant moved twice for a mistrial, alleging that the plaintiff's counsel made improper statements in cross-examination and in closing address, including references to State Farm's conduct, a "big lie," and Canada Pension Plan (CPP) benefits. The trial judge dismissed both motions, gave curative jury instructions, and awarded interest at 2% per month on overdue benefits.

ISSUES: (1) Whether the trial judge erred in dismissing the two mistrial motions and in providing inadequate jury instructions regarding counsel's improper statements. (2) Whether the trial judge erred in awarding interest at 2% per month rather than the reduced 1% rate under the 2010 regulation.

DECISION: The Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal and awarded costs to the respondent (paras 12-13).

REASONS: The trial judge's mistrial rulings were entitled to deference, and her reasons disclosed no error of law or principle; she appropriately concluded that curative instructions could undo any prejudice (paras 2-4). The jury instructions were clear and comprehensive on each contested point, including directing the jury to disregard State Farm's conduct, the "big lie," and CPP references (paras 5-7). The court further held that s. 2(2) of the 2010 Schedule preserved the substantive right to the former 2% interest rate, as it only changed procedure, not substantive rights (paras 9-10).

## 4. *Zacharias v. Zurich Insurance Company*, 2013 ONCA 482 (2013)
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/12314/index.do

FACTS: The respondent, Nancy Zacharias, was injured in a motor vehicle accident on December 18, 1990, and received weekly benefits from her insurer, Zurich, under the Statutory Accident Benefits Schedule – Accidents before January 1, 1994 (SABS 1990). Zurich terminated her benefits in 1996, and Zacharias sued for ongoing benefits and interest on overdue amounts under s. 24(4) of SABS 1990, which provides for interest at "2 per cent per month" without specifying whether it is simple or compound. Zurich moved for a determination of whether the interest is simple or compound, and the motion judge held it was compound; Zurich appealed.

ISSUES: The court considered whether the wording of s. 24(4) of SABS 1990 is ambiguous regarding the type of interest payable on overdue benefits. It also addressed whether the provision should be interpreted as providing for simple or compound interest, considering the legislative scheme and intent, and whether subsequent amendments to similar provisions (e.g., s. 68 of SABS 1994) and other statutory provisions (e.g., s. 282(10) of the Insurance Act) shed light on the interpretation.

DECISION: The court dismissed the appeal, holding that s. 24(4) of SABS 1990 provides for compound interest (para 62). The court affirmed the motion judge's conclusion but followed a different analytical route, finding the provision unambiguous and requiring compound interest (paras 44, 61).

REASONS: The court held that s. 24(4) is not ambiguous; reading the provision in its grammatical and ordinary sense, together with the scheme and intent of the legislation, leads to only one interpretation—compound interest (paras 41, 54). The court reasoned that when a benefit is overdue, interest is added to the overdue amount, and as long as that amount remains unpaid, interest accrues on both the principal and previously accumulated interest, making the interest compound (paras 43-44). The court rejected Zurich's argument that ss. 24(1) and (2) exclude interest from "overdue payments," noting those subsections only define when amounts become overdue and cannot restrict what constitutes an overdue payment (para 45). The court also distinguished the wording change in s. 68 of SABS 1994, finding it was purposive to increase effectiveness by requiring daily calculation of interest, not to change from simple to compound interest (paras 48-50).

## 5. *McLean v. Knox*, 2013 ONCA 357 (2013)
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/20928/index.do

FACTS: Kyle McLean was seriously injured when Matthew Knox, who was intoxicated, lost control of his car after both had been drinking at Finnigan’s Roadhouse. Finnigan’s admitted 1% liability before trial, and the jury assessed McLean’s damages, including $0 for future income loss, and apportioned fault at 84% Knox, 1% Finnigan’s, and 15% McLean. The trial judge set aside the jury’s $0 future income loss award and substituted his own assessment of $117,200.

ISSUES: (1) Whether the trial judge erred in disregarding the jury’s verdict of $0 for future income loss and substituting his own damages assessment. (2) Whether the trial judge erred in his costs award. (3) On cross-appeal, whether a new trial should be ordered due to defence counsel’s conduct or the complexity of the case, and whether the jury questions on apportionment of liability were flawed.

DECISION: The appeal was allowed in part: the trial judge’s substituted award for future income loss was set aside, and the jury’s verdict of $0 was restored (paras 32, 58). The costs award was upheld (para 40). The cross-appeal was dismissed on the grounds of defence conduct and jury complexity, but the court found the jury questions on apportionment were flawed (paras 47, 49, 58).

REASONS: The trial judge erred because there was evidence supporting the jury’s $0 award, including McLean’s post-accident earnings and credibility issues, so the common law test and rule 52.08 were not met (paras 23-24, 31). The trial judge’s costs award was a proper exercise of discretion, considering the factors under rule 57 and defence counsel’s conduct (paras 36-40). The trial judge’s refusal to strike the jury was entitled to deference, as he was best positioned to assess the conduct and complexity (paras 44-46). However, the jury questions were flawed because, per *Pilon v. Janveaux*, the jury should have separately apportioned Finnigan’s liability for causing the accident versus causing the damage, and the questions failed to do so (paras 54-56).

## 6. *Barrington v. The Institute of Chartered Accountants of Ontario*, 2011 ONCA 409 (2011)
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/10525/index.do

FACTS: The Institute of Chartered Accountants of Ontario (ICAO) brought professional disciplinary proceedings against three chartered accountants (Barrington, Power, and Russo) for their roles in the 1997 audit of Livent Inc., which later restated its financial statements due to irregularities (paras 1-2, 13). After a 37-day hearing, the Discipline Committee (DC) found the members guilty of professional misconduct for failing to comply with GAAP and GAAS, imposing fines and costs (paras 2-3). The Divisional Court quashed some convictions, including all against Barrington, and set aside the costs order, leading to cross-appeals (paras 5-6).

ISSUES: (1) Whether the members had adequate notice of the case they had to defend regarding charges 1(i) and (iii), particularly concerning evidence about a "Put" agreement (para 34). (2) Whether the DC provided adequate reasons for its findings on charges 1(iv) and 2(viii) (para 34). (3) Whether subsequent legislative amendments retroactively validated the DC's costs award (para 34).

DECISION: The Court of Appeal allowed the ICAO's appeal and dismissed Power and Russo's appeal, reinstating the DC's decisions, including the convictions and the costs award (para 10).

REASONS: The court held that the Divisional Court erred in finding a denial of procedural fairness because the Put was not a new allegation or essential element of charges 1(i) and (iii), but rather evidence relevant to the charge that all significant acts had not been completed (paras 42, 47-52). The court further reasoned that the DC did not find the Put itself was a breach of GAAP, but that the members recognized income without reasonable assurance that significant acts would be completed, and the members were not surprised or prejudiced (paras 53, 45). The court also found that the DC provided adequate reasons for charges 1(iv) and 2(viii), and that the legislative amendments had retroactive effect to validate the costs award (paras 34, 10).

## 7. *Revios Canada Ltd. v. Creber*, 2011 ONCA 338 (2011)
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/10429/index.do

FACTS: Gordon Creber began working for Revios Canada Ltd. in 1998 and was terminated in 2006 at age 52. He was a member of the company’s registered pension plan (the Plan) and a party to an Executive Supplementary Pension Agreement (ESPA), which provided benefits in excess of the Plan’s limits. Creber sought a declaration that he was entitled to an unreduced pension at age 62 under both documents, but the motion judge dismissed his claim.

ISSUES: The sole issue on appeal was whether the motion judge erred in interpreting the Plan and ESPA to deny Creber an unreduced pension at age 62. Specifically, the court considered whether the ESPA’s reference to “early retirement” could be linked to s. 8.04 of the Plan, which allows early commencement of deferred pensions, to create such an entitlement.

DECISION: The Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal and awarded costs to the respondent fixed at $10,000, inclusive of disbursements and taxes (para 28).

REASONS: The court held that the ESPA is supplementary to the Plan and must be read in conjunction with it, not as a standalone document (paras 14-15). The court found that “early retirement” under the Plan is defined in s. 5.02 as occurring when employment ends between ages 55 and 65, and s. 8.04 does not create an alternative early retirement date—it merely permits early commencement of a deferred pension (paras 24-26). The court reasoned that interpreting the ESPA as Creber argued would grant a benefit to executives who did not qualify for early retirement, contrary to the contractual intent (para 27).

## 8. *Loftus v. Security National Insurance Company*, 2009 ONCA 618 (2009)
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/8392/index.do

FACTS: Elizabeth Loftus was injured in a 2001 car accident when her vehicle was struck by an uninsured driver, Mr. Robertson, who was fleeing police (paras 6-7). She sued Robertson and her own insurer, Security National, under the uninsured motorist coverage of her policy (para 7). Security National brought third-party proceedings against the police and municipality, alleging their negligence contributed to the accident, but Loftus did not sue these third parties (paras 9-10). The parties agreed for a Rule 22 motion that the third parties were negligent and insured, but Loftus had not sued them (paras 10-11).

ISSUES: The central issue was whether Security National was liable to pay Loftus under the uninsured coverage provisions of her policy when an insured joint tortfeasor (the third parties) had admitted negligence but had not been sued by Loftus (paras 2-3, 11). The court also considered the meaning of "entitled to recover money" in ss. 2(1)(b) and (c) of the Uninsured Automobile Coverage Schedule (para 32).

DECISION: The court allowed the appeal in part, holding that Security National is liable to pay Loftus under the uninsured coverage unless the third parties admit they are obliged to pay her or she obtains judgment against them (para 3). The court answered the Rule 22 question in the affirmative, subject to that condition (para 3).

REASONS: The court held that the phrase "entitled to recover" in ss. 2(1)(b) and (c) of the Schedule means "entitled to recover in fact," not merely as a matter of law (paras 35, 39). An injured insured is only disentitled from uninsured coverage where a joint tortfeasor's insurer admits liability to pay or where the insured obtains judgment against the joint tortfeasor (para 40). The court reasoned that requiring insured victims to pursue potentially speculative and costly litigation against insured joint tortfeasors would undermine the "safety net" purpose of s. 265 of the Insurance Act (paras 41-42). The court further noted that the Schedule's language makes uninsured coverage an alternative to recovery from other insurers, and that a judgment against the insurer signifies an election barring subsequent claims against joint tortfeasors (paras 46-47).

## 9. *R. v. Hamilton*, C39715, C39716 (2004)
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/3828/index.do

FACTS: The respondents, Marsha Hamilton and Donna Mason, were convicted of importing cocaine after being caught smuggling pellets of cocaine they had swallowed into Canada from Jamaica (paras 4, 8, 16). At sentencing, the trial judge, on his own initiative, expanded the proceedings to examine systemic racial and gender bias, poverty, and other societal factors, ultimately imposing conditional sentences of twenty months and two years less a day respectively (paras 6, 28-31). The Crown appealed the sentences, arguing the judge overstepped his role and erred in principle (paras 32-35).

ISSUES: The primary issues were whether the trial judge erred in the conduct of the sentencing proceedings by taking on an inquisitorial role and relying on his own materials and experiences, and whether the conditional sentences were fit given the seriousness of cocaine importation and the governing appellate authorities (paras 32-35). The court also considered whether systemic racial and gender bias could properly mitigate sentences for drug couriers and whether delay and "test case" features justified conditional sentences (paras 28-31, 41-43).

DECISION: The appeal was allowed, and the conditional sentences were set aside. The court substituted sentences of two years less a day for each respondent, to be served in jail (para 7, 45). The court held that the trial judge erred in principle and that the offences merited substantial prison terms despite mitigating personal circumstances (para 7).

REASONS: The court held that sentencing must focus on the specific offence and offender, not on group characteristics or societal grievances, and that the trial judge improperly expanded the proceedings beyond their narrow focus (paras 2-3, 7). The court found that the trial judge erred by relying on systemic racial and gender bias as mitigating factors without evidentiary support linking those factors to the commission of the offences, and by using "purity-adjusted" weight and delay/test-case features to justify conditional sentences (paras 27-31, 34-35). The court further reasoned that binding authorities required substantial jail terms for cocaine importers, even couriers, and that the trial judge's approach undermined the principles of denunciation and general deterrence (paras 3, 7, 34).

## 10. *Casurina Limited Partnership v. Rio Algom Limited*, C38844 (2004)
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/4057/index.do

FACTS: The appellants held convertible debentures issued by Rio Algom Limited. When Billiton plc made a successful takeover bid for all Rio Algom shares, the shares were delisted, rendering the conversion feature worthless. The appellants brought an oppression claim against Rio Algom, its directors, and Billiton, arguing that the takeover and subsequent transfer of Rio Algom's subsidiaries unfairly disregarded their interests (paras 1, 3-5, 14-17).

ISSUES: (1) Whether the takeover and delisting of Rio Algom's shares constituted oppressive conduct against the debentureholders, given the terms of the trust indenture. (2) Whether the transfer of Rio Algom's subsidiaries to Billiton was oppressive. (3) Whether the "no-action" clause in the trust indenture barred the appellants from bringing an oppression claim individually (paras 16-18, 20-21).

DECISION: The Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal, upholding Spence J.'s decision that the oppression claim was barred by the no-action clause and that the takeover/delisting did not constitute oppression (para 1). The court did not disturb the finding that the subsidiary transfer was oppressive but held the appellants could not pursue it due to the no-action clause (paras 20-21, 45).

REASONS: The court held that the trust indenture expressly provided for the eventuality of delisting by making it an event of default with a remedy (calling debentures due at par), so the takeover did not unfairly disregard debentureholders' interests (paras 19, 31-33). The court further reasoned that the no-action clause in s. 7.6 precluded individual debentureholders from suing for any remedy, including oppression, unless they first sought action from the trustee; the clause was not limited to actions against the issuer but applied to all claims (paras 21, 38-42). The court concluded that the appellants' failure to follow the indenture's procedure barred their oppression claim (para 45).

## 11. *Partners v. Lam*, C36894 (2002)
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/2866/index.do

FACTS: The appellant solicitors represented the respondent, a quadriplegic client, under a contingency fee arrangement confirmed in an October 1996 letter (paras 5-8). After a settlement at mediation, the client signed written instructions agreeing to fees of $500,000 inclusive of GST (paras 12-15). The client later challenged the fees, and an assessment officer reduced them; the motions judge set aside the assessment and found the March 2000 fee agreement unenforceable, reducing fees further (paras 2-3, 26-29).

ISSUES: (1) Whether the March 2000 contingency fee agreement was enforceable under the Solicitors Act, specifically whether it was fair and reasonable (paras 4, 30-36). (2) If not, whether the motions judge erred in her own assessment of the fees (para 4). (3) Whether the costs awards to the client should be set aside (para 4).

DECISION: The appeal was allowed; the motions judge’s order was set aside and the matter was remitted for a new assessment of the solicitors’ fees (para 45). The court held the March 2000 fee agreement was enforceable and the motions judge erred in her analysis (paras 36, 44).

REASONS: The court held that the motions judge erred by focusing solely on the circumstances of the March 2000 mediation, rather than the entire course of dealings, and that the client fully understood the fee arrangement (paras 38-40). The court further reasoned that the fee agreement was reasonable given the risk assumed by the solicitors and the substantial recovery achieved, and that the motions judge improperly substituted her view of reasonableness (paras 41-43). The costs awards were also set aside because they were based on the erroneous finding of unenforceability (para 44).

## 12. *Transport North American Express Inc. v. New Solutions Financial Corporation*, C36517 (2002)
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/2610/index.do

FACTS: Transport North American Express Inc. obtained a $500,000 loan from New Solutions Financial Corporation, with an agreement requiring interest at 4% per month (effective annual rate of 60.10%), plus fees and royalty payments, resulting in a criminal interest rate under s. 347(1)(a) of the Criminal Code (paras 2-6). The application judge found the agreement violated s. 347 and applied "notional severance" to reduce the interest rate to 60% per annum, rather than severing the offending provisions entirely (paras 11-14, 26).

ISSUES: (1) Where a loan agreement violates s. 347(1)(a) of the Criminal Code, may a court require the debtor to comply with certain provisions of the agreement, including payment of interest? (2) If so, may the court rewrite the agreement to reduce the effective annual interest rate to 60%, or must it apply the "blue pencil" approach of severing offending provisions (para 17)?

DECISION: The appeal was allowed; the court set aside the application judge's order and remitted the matter for a new determination of the appropriate severance remedy, holding that "notional severance" was not available (paras 1, 34-35).

REASONS: The court held that severance under s. 347 is governed by established principles requiring that the offending provisions be excised without altering the substance of the remaining agreement, and that the "blue pencil" rule—though a rule of thumb—reflects the true test of whether the void part can be removed without changing the meaning of the remainder (paras 18-19, 27-29). The court further reasoned that "notional severance" improperly rewrites the parties' agreement and undermines the deterrent purpose of s. 347, as it would encourage lenders to take calculated risks knowing they would only lose interest exceeding 60% (paras 30-31). The court emphasized that while judges have discretion to sever only some interest provisions (e.g., a bonus) rather than all, they cannot reduce an interest rate to the legal maximum, as this would constitute a "substantial innovation" inconsistent with the criminal law's aims (paras 22-25, 31-33).
