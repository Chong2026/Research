## 1. *Rodriguez-Vergara v. Lamoureux*, 2025 ONCA 620
**Year:** 2025
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/23550/index.do

RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation and application to facts

SUMMARY OF OPCF 44R DISCUSSION:
- Para [2]: Describes the OPCF 44R as a Family Protection Endorsement attached to Vergara's standard automobile policy, providing coverage when the insured is involved in an accident with an underinsured motorist. This is a factual reference to the endorsement's existence and purpose.
- Para [3]: Notes the parties' agreement that the at-fault driver's $300,000 third-party liability limits are primary, but they disagree on whether the PLUP or the OPCF 44R responds next. This frames the priority dispute.
- Para [5]-[6]: Summarizes the motion judge's findings: after the $300,000 primary limits, the OPCF 44R responds next up to $700,000, followed by the PLUP; the OPCF 44R cannot deduct PLUP amounts nor subrogate against the at-fault defendants. This is an application of the OPCF 44R priority rules.
- Para [9]: Identifies the main issue: which excess policy (OPCF 44R or PLUP) responds after the primary policy limits are exhausted.
- Para [11]: Endorses the motion judge's interpretation of the OPCF 44R "in its full legal context" and finds it consistent with this court's jurisprudence.
- Para [12]-[13]: Discusses s. 7(a) of the OPCF 44R, which makes coverage "excess to" amounts available from "insurers of the inadequately insured motorist." RSA argued "insurers" should include all insurers, including the PLUP insurer. The court rejects this plain-meaning argument.
- Para [14]-[15]: Contextualizes the OPCF 44R within Ontario's highly regulated automobile insurance regime, noting the OPCF 44R attaches to a specific vehicle, whereas a PLUP is a different kind of excess policy that does not attach to a vehicle and is not a motor vehicle insurance policy (citing Keelty v. Bernique).
- Para [16]: Sets out the full text of ss. 4 and 7 of the OPCF 44R, including the priority provisions in s. 7(a)-(i).
- Para [17]: Holds that s. 7(a)'s reference to "insurers of the inadequately insured motorist" means amounts from motor vehicle liability insurance or funds in lieu of insurance, not any and all types of insurance such as a PLUP. This is a substantive interpretation of s. 7(a).
- Para [18]: Supports the interpretation by analogy to Smith v. Taylor, 2024 ONCA 223, which held that similar priority language in s. 18(a)(ii) of the OPCF 44R includes only motor vehicle insurance policies, not homeowner's excess liability endorsements. The court states s. 7(a) "governs priorities between the OPCF 44R and other insurance policies" and the motion judge correctly applied Smith.
- Para [19]: Finds the interpretation consistent with s. 4 of the OPCF 44R, which limits maximum liability by reference to "the total of all limits of motor vehicle liability insurance" — it would be nonsensical for s. 4 to include only motor vehicle policies while s. 7 ranked behind any insurance policy.
- Para [20]: Confirms the PLUP responds only after underlying motor vehicle policy limits are exhausted, citing Benson v. Walt, 2018 ONCA 172, which held a PLUP does not fall within the priority scheme of motor vehicle insurance policies under the Insurance Act.
- Para [21]: Holds the OPCF 44R insurer cannot deduct PLUP amounts from its payment because the PLUP is not within s. 7(a); acceding to the appellant's argument would render the automobile insurance regulatory sections meaningless.
- Para [22]: Rejects RSA's attempt to subrogate against the at-fault defendants or issue a third-party claim against Certas for amounts paid under the OPCF 44R, calling it an attempt to "get through the back door of subrogation what it cannot get through the front door of priority." The court notes the OPCF 44R can subrogate against the at-fault party to the extent their liability exceeds the $300,000 limit and the PLUP's limit.

## 2. *Smith v. Taylor*, 2024 ONCA 223
**Year:** 2024
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/22207/index.do

RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation and application to facts

SUMMARY OF OPCF 44R DISCUSSION:
- Para [6]: The court describes the OPCF 44R as the standard Ontario Policy Change Form 44R, which is optional family protection coverage added to the standard Ontario Automobile Policy (OAP1). It notes that the OPCF 44R indemnifies the insured for up to $1 million if an insured is injured or killed by an "inadequately insured motorist," defined as a third-party motorist with "total motor vehicle liability insurance" less than the "limit of family protection coverage" provided by the policyholder's OPCF 44R.
- Para [9]: The court notes that the appellant acknowledged she was not entitled to payment under the OPCF 44R because the $1 million indemnity under the OPCF 44R equals the $1 million indemnity under the tortfeasor's policy, meaning the tortfeasor is not an "inadequately insured motorist" under the OPCF 44R.
- Para [11]-[12]: The court summarizes the motion judge's interpretation, which held that the Excess Endorsement required the tortfeasor to be an "inadequately insured motorist" as defined in the OPCF 44R, and that the insured had to exhaust the OPCF 44R limits before recovering under the Excess Endorsement.
- Para [16]: The court sets out the relevant provisions of the OPCF 44R, including the definitions of "family protection coverage" (s. 1.4), "inadequately insured motorist" (s. 1.5), "limit of family protection coverage" (s. 1.7), and the multiple coverages rules (s. 18), which provide that first loss insurance must be exhausted before recourse to excess insurance.
- Para [19]-[20]: The court summarizes the motion judge's reasoning that the definition of "inadequately insured motorist" in the OPCF 44R applied to the Excess Endorsement because there was no language in the Excess Endorsement modifying that term, and because the Excess Endorsement was "subject to all the same terms, conditions, limitations and exclusions as the Underlying Policy."
- Para [21]: The court notes the motion judge relied on paras. 18(a)(ii) and (c) of the OPCF 44R to conclude that the insured had to exhaust the OPCF 44R limits before recovering under the Excess Endorsement.
- Para [27]-[28]: The court identifies the motion judge's error in applying the OPCF 44R definition of "inadequately insured motorist" to the Excess Endorsement, holding that this interpretation would convert the Excess Endorsement into a second OPCF 44R policy and undermine the purpose of the Excess Endorsement, which is to provide coverage in excess of the OPCF 44R.

## 4. *Rooplal v. Fodor*, 2021 ONCA 357
**Year:** 2021
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/19684/index.do

RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation and application to facts

SUMMARY OF OPCF 44R DISCUSSION:
- Para [14]: The court notes that at the time of the accident, Ms. Rooplal had a valid motor vehicle insurance policy with Novex, which included a family protection coverage endorsement under OPCF-44R. This is a factual reference establishing the existence of the endorsement.
- Para [15]: The court recounts that Ms. Rooplal's Statement of Claim sought a declaration and indemnification from Novex "up to the full policy limits, including but not limited to OPCF-44R family protection coverage, for any and all damages, injuries and losses resulting from the motor vehicle accident." This is an application of the endorsement to the pleadings.
- Para [16]: The court notes that Ms. Rooplal claimed that her Family Protection Coverage endorsement with Novex, under the OPCF-44R, "provides coverage to persons insured under the contract for loss and injuries sustained as a result of the actions of an unknown operator of an unidentified motor vehicle pursuant to section 265(1) of the Insurance Act." This describes the legal basis of the claim under the endorsement.
- Paras [29]-[31]: The court provides a substantive interpretation of OPCF-44R, explaining that it is an optional endorsement to the standard Ontario motor vehicle insurance Owner's Policy that provides additional coverage for the named insured and their family, including for unidentified motorist claims. The court emphasizes that under s. 7 of the OPCF-44R, coverage under this policy is excess to any amount received elsewhere and to other amounts available from uninsured automobile coverage under any other valid motor vehicle policy. This is a substantive interpretation of the endorsement's scope and priority.
- Para [40]: The court discusses Schmitz v. Lombard General Insurance Company of Canada, 2014 ONCA 88, a case involving an insured's claim against their own insurer under the OPCF-44R endorsement. The court notes that in Schmitz, this court held that the reasoning in Markel was "dispositive" of the limitations issue such that a claim for indemnity under the OPCF-44R did not start to run until there is a demand for indemnity and default of the obligation to indemnify the insured. This is a substantive interpretation of when the limitation period begins for OPCF-44R claims.
- Para [42]: The court notes that the motion judge held that, although this is not a claim against the OPCF-44R insurer, the analysis in Markel and Schmitz applies. The motion judge concluded that Ms. Rooplal could not be said to know that there is a loss "caused" by an "omission" of the unidentified motorist insurer until she asserted a claim for indemnification against TTC Insurance. This applies the OPCF-44R limitation principles to the s. 265 insurer context.

KEY HOLDING ON OPCF 44R: The court confirms that the limitation period for a claim for indemnification under OPCF-44R (and by extension, s. 265 unidentified motorist coverage) does not begin to run until the insured makes a demand for indemnification and the insurer defaults on that obligation, applying the reasoning from Markel and Schmitz.

## 7. *Murphy v. Savoie*, 2019 ONCA 784
**Year:** 2019
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/18235/index.do

RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation and application to facts

SUMMARY OF OPCF 44R DISCUSSION:
- Para [1]: The court notes that the appellant Aviva provided automobile insurance to Emblem Flowers that includes an OPCF 44R Family Protection Coverage endorsement, establishing the policy context.
- Para [5]: The court quotes the key provision of the endorsement, s. 1.6(b), which defines an "insured person" where the named insured is a corporation, including "any officer, employee or partner of the named insured for whose regular use the described automobile is provided" and their spouse, while not an occupant of an automobile and struck by an automobile.
- Para [6]: The court quotes s. 2 of the endorsement, which provides that "[t]he definitions in section 1 apply as of the time of the happening of an accident for which indemnity is provided under this change form."
- Para [7]: The court records Aviva's argument that, properly construing s. 2, Ms. Burke Murphy would only be covered if Mr. Hogan was "on duty" at the very moment of the accident—i.e., that the "temporal nexus" must be precise.
- Para [8]: The court summarizes the motion judge's reasoning, which held that the van was available for Mr. Hogan's use at the time of the accident because Emblem Flowers had opened at 9:00 a.m. and Mr. Hogan had full discretionary access to the van during business hours, even though he had not yet arrived at work when the accident occurred at approximately 9:30 a.m.
- Para [9]-[12]: The court rejects Aviva's invitation to apply the "considerable jurisprudence" construing s. 66 of O.Reg. 403/96 (the SABS deemed named insured provision), noting that the SABS provision serves a different purpose (priority disputes among insurers) than the endorsement (extending coverage to insureds), and that the language of the two provisions is not similar.
- Para [13]: The court emphasizes the interpretive context: automobile insurance policies are part of an "integral social safety net" and the extension of spousal protection in the endorsement has a consumer protection purpose.
- Para [14]-[15]: The court rejects Aviva's argument that the "temporal nexus" must be that the employee was "on duty" at the precise moment of the accident.
- Para [16]: The court holds that s. 2 requires that "at the time of the accident," the employee must be one "for whose regular use the described automobile is provided"—and that this is a sufficient temporal nexus. Because the van was available for Mr. Hogan's "regular use" any time after the business opened, the requirement was satisfied.
- Para [17]: The court notes the odd results that would follow from Aviva's interpretation (e.g., coverage would depend on whether the employee arrived early or was late due to a punctured tire), and concludes that no words in the endorsement compel denial of coverage on the facts.

KEY HOLDING ON OPCF 44R: The court held that under s. 2 of OPCF 44R, the temporal nexus required is that the employee be one "for whose regular use the described automobile is provided" at the time of the accident—not that the employee be "on duty" at the precise moment of the accident—so a spouse is covered where the employer's vehicle was available for the employee's regular use during business hours, even if the employee had not yet arrived at work when the accident occurred.

## 8. *Kahlon v. ACE INA Insurance*, 2019 ONCA 774
**Year:** 2019
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/18224/index.do

RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation and application to facts

SUMMARY OF OPCF 44R DISCUSSION:

- **Para [4]**: Describes the OPCF 44R Family Protection Coverage endorsement as providing insureds with coverage for a collision with an underinsured vehicle, with recovery limited to the amount by which the insured's family protection limit exceeds the underinsured motorist's liability limit.

- **Para [20]-[23]**: Distinguishes between mandatory uninsured automobile coverage (under s. 265 of the Insurance Act and the policy) and optional underinsured motorist coverage under OPCF 44R, which is not mandatory and can be purchased by the insured.

- **Para [27]-[31]**: Holds that an insurer may validly tailor its OPCF 44R coverage through an endorsement (here, endorsement 11A) that limits coverage to private passenger and light commercial vehicles and excludes heavy commercial vehicles over 4,500 kg. Such an endorsement is enforceable even without Superintendent approval, applying Royal & Sun Alliance. An insurer is not legally required to provide underinsured coverage and may precisely tailor its offering.

- **Para [32]**: States that even without endorsement 11A, the court would have reached the same conclusion based on its interpretation of the policy and the endorsement (developed in the subsequent paragraphs).

- **Para [41]-[42]**: Discusses interpretation principles for OPCF 44R (and its predecessor S.E.F. 44), citing Chilton: ambiguities in the endorsement are construed against the insurer because the insurance industry drafts these standard forms and insureds have no bargaining power.

- **Para [43]-[45]**: Holds that the OPCF 44R endorsement is not a standalone policy; it is linked to and built on the foundation of the underlying automobile policy. However, the endorsement has a "mobility element" – coverage is portable and follows the insured person regardless of which vehicle they are driving, subject to applicable exclusions.

- **Para [46]-[50]**: Sets out the text of the endorsement, including s. 3 (indemnity for compensatory damages arising from use/operation of an automobile), the definition of "insured person," and critically s. 22, which provides that all limits, terms, conditions, provisions, definitions and exclusions of the policy have full force and effect unless otherwise provided in the endorsement.

- **Para [51]-[52] and following (implied)**: Notes that the policy's s. 2.2.3 extends coverages to "Other Automobiles" and that the policy contains exclusions (including for heavy commercial vehicles over 4,500 kg) that, through s. 22 of the endorsement, apply to the OPCF 44R coverage.

- **Para [53]-[60] (inferred from context)**: The court ultimately holds that the policy's exclusions, incorporated through s. 22 of the endorsement, apply to the OPCF 44R coverage, so a truck driver injured while operating a heavy commercial vehicle cannot access underinsured coverage under his personal automobile policy.

KEY HOLDING ON OPCF 44R: The OPCF 44R endorsement is not a standalone policy; through s. 22, all exclusions in the underlying automobile policy apply to the underinsured coverage, so a driver injured while operating a heavy commercial vehicle (over 4,500 kg) is excluded from coverage under his personal automobile policy's OPCF 44R endorsement.

## 13. *Skunk v. Ketash*, 2018 ONCA 450
**Year:** 2018
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/16821/index.do

RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation

SUMMARY OF OPCF 44R DISCUSSION:
- Para [4]: The court quotes s. 1.11 of the OPCF 44R Family Protection Coverage, noting it reads in virtually identical terms to s. 265(2) of the Insurance Act and s. 5.1.2 of the OAP, defining an "uninsured automobile" as not including "an automobile owned by or registered in the name of the insured or his or her spouse."
- Para [5]: The court notes the motion judge found no ambiguity in the plain language of the OPCF 44R (along with the statute and OAP) and concluded the language is clear on its face.
