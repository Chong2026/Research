## 1. *Rodriguez-Vergara v. Lamoureux*, 2025 ONCA 620
**Year:** 2025
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/23550/index.do

FACTS: The plaintiff Vergara was struck by a vehicle driven by Lamoureux and owned by D'Souza, whose automobile policy had $300,000 liability limits. D'Souza also had a $1 million personal umbrella liability policy (PLUP) with Certas, while Vergara had a $1 million OPCF 44R endorsement with RSA. The parties disputed which policy responded after the $300,000 primary limits were exhausted.

ISSUES: The court addressed whether Vergara's OPCF 44R or D'Souza's PLUP ranked first in priority after the primary policy limits were paid, whether RSA could deduct the PLUP limits from its OPCF 44R payment, and whether RSA could subrogate against Certas or issue a third-party claim.

DECISION: The court dismissed the appeal, affirming the motion judge's order that Vergara's OPCF 44R responded next up to $700,000, followed by D'Souza's PLUP (para 24).

REASONS: The court held that s. 7(a) of the OPCF 44R, referring to "insurers of the inadequately insured motorist," includes only motor vehicle liability insurers, not PLUPs, which fall outside Ontario's motor vehicle insurance regime (paras 14-17). The court reasoned that this interpretation is consistent with s. 4 of the OPCF 44R and prior jurisprudence including *Smith v. Taylor* and *Benson v. Walt* (paras 18-20). The court further held that RSA could not deduct the PLUP limits or subrogate against Certas, as this would circumvent the priority scheme (paras 21-22).

OPCF 44R ANALYSIS:
RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation — the court directly interpreted key provisions of the OPCF 44R endorsement.

PASSAGES:
- Para [4]: Describes the OPCF 44R as providing coverage if the insured is involved in an accident with an underinsured motorist.
- Para [12]-[13]: Sets out RSA's argument that s. 7(a) of the OPCF 44R, referring to "insurers of the inadequately insured motorist," should include all insurers, including PLUP insurers, based on plain meaning.
- Para [14]-[15]: The court rejects RSA's plain meaning argument, holding that the OPCF 44R must be interpreted in the context of Ontario's highly regulated automobile insurance regime, and that a PLUP is not a motor vehicle insurance policy.
- Para [16]: Quotes the full text of ss. 4 and 7 of the OPCF 44R, including the priority provisions.
- Para [17]: Holds that s. 7 refers to matters covered in automobile regulations or motor vehicle liability policies, and that "insurers of the inadequately insured motorist" means amounts from total motor vehicle liability insurance, not any and all types of insurance such as a PLUP.
- Para [18]: Applies *Smith v. Taylor*, 2024 ONCA 223, which held that similar language in s. 18(a)(ii) of the OPCF 44R included only motor vehicle insurance policies, not homeowner's excess liability endorsements.
- Para [19]: Holds that interpreting s. 7(a) to include PLUPs would be inconsistent with s. 4, which limits maximum coverage by reference to "motor vehicle liability insurance."
- Para [20]: Confirms that a PLUP responds only after motor vehicle liability policy limits are exhausted, citing *Benson v. Walt*, 2018 ONCA 172.
- Para [21]: Holds that RSA could not deduct the PLUP limits from its OPCF 44R payment because the PLUP does not fall within s. 7(a).
- Para [22]: Holds that RSA could not subrogate against Certas or the defendants for amounts paid under the OPCF 44R, as this would circumvent the priority scheme.

KEY HOLDING ON OPCF 44R (if any): The court held that s. 7(a) of the OPCF 44R, referring to "insurers of the inadequately insured motorist," includes only motor vehicle liability insurers, not personal umbrella liability policies, which therefore rank after the OPCF 44R in priority.

## 2. *Smith v. Taylor*, 2024 ONCA 223
**Year:** 2024
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/22207/index.do

FACTS: Kerry Smith sought coverage under a homeowners policy's "Personal Excess Liability Policy" endorsement after her common-law spouse was killed by Robert Taylor, whose auto insurer (Wawanesa) provided $1 million in liability coverage. Smith's own auto policy included OPCF 44R family protection coverage with a $1 million limit, but she conceded she could not recover under it because Taylor was not an "inadequately insured motorist" (his coverage matched her OPCF 44R limit). Aviva denied coverage under the Excess Endorsement, and the motion judge dismissed the action against Aviva, holding that the OPCF 44R definition of "inadequately insured motorist" applied to the Excess Endorsement and that Smith had to exhaust OPCF 44R limits first (paras 2-13).

ISSUES: (1) Did the motion judge err in interpreting the Excess Endorsement by importing the OPCF 44R definition of "inadequately insured motorist" into the Excess Endorsement? (2) Did the motion judge err in requiring exhaustion of OPCF 44R limits before recourse to the Excess Endorsement? (3) Should the order be set aside as nullifying coverage or being commercially unreasonable (paras 22-24)?

DECISION: The appeal was allowed; the motion judge's order dismissing the action against Aviva was set aside (para 15, 52). The court held that the Excess Endorsement provides coverage stacking on top of the OPCF 44R limits and that Smith could claim under it without exhausting OPCF 44R coverage (paras 45-51).

REASONS: The court held that the motion judge erred in applying the OPCF 44R definition of "inadequately insured motorist" to the Excess Endorsement because doing so undermined the Excess Endorsement's purpose of providing additional coverage beyond the OPCF 44R (paras 28-29). The court distinguished Kahlon v. ACE INA Insurance, 2019 ONCA 774, noting that adopting the underlying definition there raised no inconsistency, unlike here (para 31). The court reasoned that the Excess Endorsement's definition of "Family Protection Coverage" expressly includes "any similar indemnity provided under any other contract of insurance," which includes the Excess Endorsement itself, indicating stacking (paras 32-34). The court further held that the OPCF 44R definition refers to the "limit of family protection coverage" in the Certificate of Automobile Insurance, which cannot include excess coverage outside the auto insurance regulatory regime (paras 36-37). The court also rejected reliance on the Insuring Agreement provision, which relates to liability coverage, not family protection coverage (paras 39-44). The court interpreted "inadequately insured motorist" in the Excess Endorsement by its plain meaning—a motorist whose insurance is insufficient to pay the damages claimed—and found Taylor was such a motorist given Smith's $3.5 million claim (paras 45-48). The second paragraph of the FPC provision confirms the Excess Endorsement pays "in excess of" the OPCF 44R (paras 50-51).

RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation—the court directly interpreted the OPCF 44R definition of "inadequately insured motorist" and its interplay with the Excess Endorsement.

- Para 6: Describes the OPCF 44R as standard Ontario Policy Change Form 44R, indemnifying up to $1 million if an insured is injured by an "inadequately insured motorist," defined as a third-party motorist with total liability insurance less than the limit of family protection coverage.
- Para 9: Notes Smith conceded she could not recover under OPCF 44R because Taylor's $1 million coverage equaled her OPCF 44R limit, so he was not an "inadequately insured motorist."
- Para 16: Sets out the OPCF 44R definitions, including "family protection coverage" (1.4), "inadequately insured motorist" (1.5), "limit of family protection coverage" (1.7), and the "Multiple Coverages" rule (para 18) requiring first loss insurance to be exhausted before excess insurance.

## 3. *McEwen (Re)*, 2021 ONCA 566
**Year:** 2021
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/19876/index.do

FACTS: The Carrolls were injured in a motor vehicle accident caused by the McEwens, who were insured by Traders for $1 million. After the McEwens declared bankruptcy, the Carrolls obtained a judgment exceeding the policy limits, and the trustee assigned the Carrolls a bad faith claim against Traders under s. 38 of the BIA. Traders moved to set aside the s. 38 order, arguing it lacked standing to challenge the assignment.

ISSUES: The primary issue was whether Traders, as the proposed defendant to the assigned bad faith action, had standing to challenge the s. 38 order. Secondary issues were whether the Carrolls were creditors of the bankrupt estate and whether the bad faith claim was property of the bankrupts that could be assigned.

DECISION: The court dismissed the appeal, holding that Traders had no standing to challenge the s. 38 order (para 38).

REASONS: The court affirmed the general rule that a proposed defendant has no standing to contest a s. 38 order, subject to limited exceptions such as abuse of process, non-disclosure, or fraud (paras 28-29). The court rejected Traders' argument that Isabelle v. Royal Bank of Canada created a new exception for "discrete and genuine issues of law," finding that Isabelle only addressed standing at the s. 38 motion itself, not post-order review (paras 32-36). The court also noted that the question of whether the Carrolls were creditors was a factual finding reviewable on a balance of probabilities, and there was evidence supporting that finding (paras 41-43).

OPCF 44R ANALYSIS:
RELEVANCE: Passing reference

PASSAGES:
- Para [8]: The court noted that the formal judgment ordered Pilot Insurance Company to pay $1 million of the judgment pursuant to the OPCF 44R endorsement in the Carrolls' own automobile insurance policy.
- Para [15]: The court described the theory of the bad faith action, which alleged that Aviva Canada (as the McEwens' insurer) improperly withheld policy limits to leverage its settlement position as the OPCF 44R insurer of Barbara Carroll.

KEY HOLDING ON OPCF 44R (if any): None. The court did not interpret or apply OPCF 44R substantively; it merely referenced the endorsement in describing the factual background and the theory of the bad faith claim.

## 4. *Rooplal v. Fodor*, 2021 ONCA 357
**Year:** 2021
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/19684/index.do

FACTS: The respondent was injured while riding a TTC bus when it braked sharply to avoid a collision with an unidentified motorist (para 12). She sued the unidentified motorist, the TTC, the bus driver, and her own insurer (Novex) under her OPCF 44R endorsement, and later sought to add TTC Insurance as a defendant for s. 265 unidentified motorist coverage (paras 2, 15-21). TTC Insurance argued the claim was statute-barred under the Limitations Act, 2002 (para 3).

ISSUES: The sole issue was when the two-year limitation period began to run on the claim for indemnification against TTC Insurance under s. 265 of the Insurance Act (paras 7, 35). Specifically, whether the claim was discovered when the respondent knew of the unidentified motorist's fault, or only when the insurer denied a demand for indemnification (paras 8-9).

DECISION: The court dismissed the appeal, holding that the limitation period for the claim against TTC Insurance had not expired (paras 10, 76).

REASONS: The court held that the reasoning in Markel and Schmitz applies to claims against a s. 265 insurer, such that the limitation period does not begin to run until the insured makes a demand for indemnification and the insurer defaults (paras 55-60). The court rejected the argument that this creates an absurd result, noting that the insured must comply with statutory notice requirements and that the insurer can rely on the discoverability principle where appropriate (paras 61-66). The court further held that the motion judge's conclusion was correct because the respondent had not yet made a demand on TTC Insurance (paras 67-75).

OPCF 44R ANALYSIS:
RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation – the court discusses OPCF 44R in the context of distinguishing it from mandatory s. 265 coverage, but the limitation analysis applies equally to both.

PASSAGES:
- Para 14: Describes that the respondent had OPCF 44R family protection coverage with Novex, while TTC was self-insured by TTC Insurance.
- Para 29-31: Explains that OPCF 44R is optional additional coverage for named insureds and family, including for unidentified motorist claims, and that s. 7 of the OPCF 44R makes coverage excess to other available amounts, including s. 265 uninsured automobile coverage.
- Para 40: Notes that in Schmitz, the court held that the Markel reasoning was "dispositive" of the limitations issue for a claim under the OPCF 44R endorsement, such that the limitation period did not start until there was a demand for indemnity and default.

KEY HOLDING ON OPCF 44R (if any): The court did not make a distinct holding on OPCF 44R itself, but confirmed that the limitation period for claims under both OPCF 44R and s. 265 begins only upon a demand for indemnification and the insurer's default (paras 55-60).

## 5. *McEwen (Re)*, 2020 ONCA 511
**Year:** 2020
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/18976/index.do

FACTS: The Carroll Plaintiffs were injured in a motor vehicle accident and sued the McEwens, who were insured by Traders. The McEwens declared bankruptcy and were discharged. After a jury trial, judgment was issued against the McEwens limited to their $1 million policy limit, with Pilot (not Traders) liable for OPCF 44R coverage. The Carroll Plaintiffs then obtained a BIA s. 38 order and assignment from the trustee to pursue a bad faith claim against Traders for failing to settle within policy limits.

ISSUES: The primary issues were (1) whether the panel had jurisdiction to review the Chambers Judge's refusal of leave to appeal under BIA s. 193(e), and (2) if so, whether leave should be granted. The underlying substantive issues included whether the bad faith claim was property of the bankrupts' estate and whether the Carroll Plaintiffs were creditors.

DECISION: The court held it had jurisdiction to consider the CJA s. 7(5) motion, set aside the Chambers Judge's order, and granted Traders leave to appeal the 2019 Review Order (paras 2, 47-48).

REASONS: The court found that the Chambers Judge erred by failing to consider whether the motion judge's decision raised a "discrete issue of law" that could avoid the need for a lawsuit, which was a recognized exception to the general rule that proposed defendants lack standing (paras 36, 84). The court reasoned that the motion judge had not addressed this argument, and the Chambers Judge's refusal of leave was based on an incomplete assessment of the merits (paras 44-45).

OPCF 44R ANALYSIS:
RELEVANCE: Passing reference

PASSAGES:
- Para [6]: The court noted that Pilot, not Aviva/Traders, insured the Carroll Plaintiffs with OPCF 44R coverage, correcting the misidentification in the original action.
- Para [16]: The court described the Carroll Plaintiffs' proposed judgment language that would make Pilot and Aviva jointly liable for $1 million pursuant to the OPCF 44R endorsement, though the judgment as issued limited liability to Pilot.
- Para [29]: The court noted the bad faith claim alleged Traders improperly adjusted claims jointly with claims against Traders as OPCF 44R insurer of Barbara Carroll, but clarified that Pilot, not Aviva/Traders, provided that coverage.

KEY HOLDING ON OPCF 44R (if any): None. The court did not interpret or apply OPCF 44R substantively; it only referenced the endorsement to clarify which insurer provided the coverage.

## 6. *Tuffnail v. Meekes*, 2020 ONCA 340
**Year:** 2020
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/18806/index.do

FACTS: Gregory Tuffnail was seriously injured in a single-vehicle crash after a wedding reception. He sued the driver (Meekes), the host (Bolton), and his own insurer (State Farm) seeking a declaration of coverage under the OPCF 44R underinsured motorist endorsement. The jury apportioned liability among Meekes (65%), Bolton (20.03%), Coulthard the bartender (11.12%), and Tuffnail himself (3.85%). Tuffnail did not sue Coulthard directly, but State Farm brought a third party claim against Coulthard for contribution and indemnity.

ISSUES: The primary issue was whether Coulthard's $1,000,000 insurance coverage was "available" to Tuffnail within the meaning of s. 7(b) of the OPCF 44R, which would reduce State Farm's liability under the endorsement. A related issue was whether State Farm's third party claim against Coulthard was effectively a subrogated claim that put Coulthard's liability to Tuffnail in issue.

DECISION: The court allowed State Farm's appeal on the OPCF 44R issue, holding that Coulthard's insurance coverage was "available" to Tuffnail under s. 7(b), and State Farm was entitled to deduct the limits of Coulthard's policy in calculating its liability (paras 24, 27). The court dismissed the other appeals and cross-appeals (para 51).

REASONS: The court held that State Farm's third party claim against Coulthard was in substance a subrogated claim advancing Tuffnail's cause of action against Coulthard (paras 31-38). Because the right of subrogation is derivative, State Farm could only bring the claim if Tuffnail had a legally enforceable claim against Coulthard (paras 37-38). The court reasoned that by bringing the subrogated claim and obtaining a jury finding of Coulthard's liability, Coulthard was "jointly liable" with Meekes within the meaning of s. 7(b) (paras 48-49). The court distinguished the trial judge's finding that Coulthard was severally liable to Bolton, noting that State Farm's claim was different in nature from Bolton's contribution claim (paras 29-30).

OPCF 44R ANALYSIS:
RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation

PASSAGES:
- Para [14]: Sets out the text of s. 7 of the OPCF 44R, which provides that the amount payable is "excess to amounts that were available to the eligible claimant from... the insurers of a person jointly liable with the inadequately insured motorist."
- Para [15]-[16]: Describes the dispute over whether Coulthard's coverage was "available" to Tuffnail, and State Farm's argument that an insured can only resort to OPCF 44R as a matter of last resort.
- Para [17]-[18]: Notes the trial judge's finding that because Tuffnail had not sued Coulthard, the proceeds were not "available," and her reliance on the change in language from the prior SEF 44 endorsement which had included "whether such entitlement is pursued or not."
- Para [24]: Holds that Coulthard is "jointly liable" to Tuffnail within the meaning of s. 7(b), his insurance is "available," and State Farm is entitled to deduct the limits of his coverage.
- Para [26]-[27]: Explains that the trial judge's finding that State Farm had a right of subrogation under s. 20 of the OPCF 44R necessarily meant Coulthard was jointly liable with Meekes, and therefore s. 7 permits the deduction.
- Para [35]: Quotes s. 20 of the OPCF 44R, which permits the insurer to subrogate against "the inadequately insured motorist and the persons referred to in section 7."
- Para [48]-[49]: Concludes that the nature of State Farm's subrogated action, the trial judge's determination that State Farm could bring it, and the jury's finding of Coulthard's liability compel the conclusion that Coulthard is jointly liable with Meekes within the meaning of s. 7.

## 7. *Murphy v. Savoie*, 2019 ONCA 784
**Year:** 2019
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/18235/index.do

FACTS: Emblem Flowers employed Colm Hogan as a delivery van driver. His spouse, Emeralda Burke Murphy, was struck by an uninsured driver (Benjamin Savoie) in a stolen car on September 25, 2014, just before 9:30 a.m., shortly before Hogan arrived at work. Aviva insured Emblem Flowers under a policy including an OPCF 44R Family Protection Coverage endorsement, and sought a determination that the endorsement did not cover Burke Murphy because Hogan was not yet "on duty" at the moment of the accident (paras 1-4).

ISSUES: Whether Burke Murphy was an "insured person" under s. 1.6(b) of the OPCF 44R endorsement, which extends coverage to the spouse of an employee "for whose regular use the described automobile is provided." Specifically, whether s. 2 of the endorsement, requiring the definitions to apply "as of the time of the happening of an accident," requires that the employee be "on duty" at the precise moment of the accident (paras 5-7, 14).

DECISION: The Court of Appeal dismissed Aviva's appeal, affirming the motion judge's ruling that Burke Murphy was covered under the OPCF 44R endorsement (para 18).

REASONS: The court rejected Aviva's argument that SABS jurisprudence under s. 66 of O.Reg. 403/96 should guide interpretation, noting the different purposes of priority rules versus coverage extension (paras 12-13). The court held that s. 2 requires only that, "at the time of the accident," the employee be one "for whose regular use the described automobile is provided," which was satisfied because the van was available for Hogan's regular use once the business opened at 9:00 a.m. (paras 16-17). The court found Aviva's "temporal nexus" argument would lead to odd results, such as coverage depending on whether Hogan arrived early or was delayed by a punctured bicycle tire (para 17).

OPCF 44R ANALYSIS:
RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation — the court directly interpreted the OPCF 44R endorsement's coverage provisions.

PASSAGES:
- Para 5: Quoted s. 1.6(b) of the OPCF 44R endorsement, defining "insured person" to include an employee of a corporate named insured "for whose regular use the described automobile is provided" and his or her spouse, while not an occupant of an automobile and struck by an automobile.
- Para 6: Quoted s. 2 of the endorsement, providing that the definitions apply "as of the time of the happening of an accident for which indemnity is provided."
- Para 7: Noted Aviva's argument that s. 2 requires the employee to be "on duty" at the very moment of the accident for spousal coverage to apply.
- Para 8: Summarized the motion judge's reasoning that the van was available for Hogan's use at the time of the accident, drawing an analogy to Old Republic, and that Hogan's failure to arrive early did not negate the vehicle's availability.
- Para 12: Rejected the usefulness of SABS jurisprudence because the purpose of the endorsement is to extend coverage, not to determine priority among insurers.
- Para 13: Emphasized that automobile insurance policies form part of an "integral social safety net" and that the extension of spousal protection in the endorsement serves this purpose.
- Para 14: Restated Aviva's argument that coverage requires a precise "temporal nexus" — that Hogan was "on duty" at the moment of the accident.
- Para 16: Held that s. 2 requires only that, at the time of the accident, the employee be one "for whose regular use the described automobile is provided," which is a sufficient temporal nexus; the van was available for Hogan's regular use after the business opened.
- Para 17: Rejected Aviva's argument as leading to arbitrary results, noting coverage would depend on whether Hogan arrived early or was delayed; no words in the endorsement compel denial of coverage on the facts.

KEY HOLDING ON OPCF 44R (if any): The court held that s. 2 of the OPCF 44R endorsement does not require the employee to be "on duty" at the precise moment of the accident; it is sufficient that the described automobile was provided for the employee's regular use at the time of the accident, which is satisfied where the vehicle was available for the employee's discretionary use during business hours (paras 16-17).

## 8. *Kahlon v. ACE INA Insurance*, 2019 ONCA 774
**Year:** 2019
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/18224/index.do

FACTS: Mr. Kahlon, a truck driver, was injured in Florida when struck by an underinsured vehicle while operating a commercial tractor owned by his corporation and leased to Bell City Transport, which had fleet insurance through ACE INA. He sought underinsured motorist coverage under both the ACE INA fleet policy and his personal automobile policy with AllState, which included OPCF 44R Family Protection Coverage endorsements (paras 1-4).

ISSUES: The court addressed two issues: (1) whether ACE INA was obliged to provide underinsured coverage under its fleet policy, given an endorsement (11A) excluding coverage for heavy commercial vehicles; and (2) whether AllState was obliged to provide underinsured coverage under Mr. Kahlon's personal policy, despite policy exclusions for commercial vehicles (paras 19, 26, 33-35).

DECISION: The court allowed AllState's appeal and dismissed Mr. Kahlon's cross-appeal, holding that neither ACE INA nor AllState was obliged to provide underinsured coverage (para 9).

REASONS: The court held that ACE INA's endorsement 11A, which excluded coverage for vehicles over 4,500 kg, was enforceable despite lack of Superintendent approval, relying on Royal & Sun Alliance (paras 30-31). Regarding AllState, the court reasoned that the OPCF 44R endorsement is not a standalone policy but is linked to the underlying policy, and s. 22 of the endorsement incorporates all policy exclusions, including those for commercial vehicles (paras 43-44, 49-50).

OPCF 44R ANALYSIS:
RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation — the court extensively analyzed the relationship between OPCF 44R and the underlying automobile policy, and the effect of policy exclusions on the endorsement.

PASSAGES:
- Para [4]: Describes the OPCF 44R endorsement's core coverage: "the insured person's coverage for injury from a collision with an underinsured automobile is 'the amount that he or she is legally entitled to recover … as compensatory damages in respect of bodily injury … or death … arising directly or indirectly from the use or operation of [the] automobile'."
- Para [23]: Distinguishes underinsured coverage under OPCF 44R from mandatory uninsured coverage: "The second situation – in which the collision is with an underinsured automobile – is addressed by the endorsement. This is optional insurance coverage, not mandatory."
- Para [43]: Holds that the endorsement is not standalone: "the endorsement is generally not understood to be a self-contained policy… it is built on the foundation of the policy and does not have an independent existence."
- Para [44]: Notes key difference: "the coverage in the policy is mandatory for insureds and insurers, while underinsured coverage under the endorsement is optional for both insureds and insurers."
- Para [45]: Discusses the "mobility element" of the endorsement: "the current form of coverage for named insureds and their families is portable, following them as passengers of other cars and as pedestrians."
- Para [49]: Quotes s. 22 of the endorsement: "Except as otherwise provided in this change form, all limits, terms, conditions, provisions, definitions and exclusions of the policy shall have full force and effect."

KEY HOLDING ON OPCF 44R (if any): The OPCF 44R endorsement is not a standalone contract; it incorporates all exclusions from the underlying automobile policy through s. 22, so policy exclusions for commercial vehicles apply to and can preclude underinsured coverage under the endorsement.

## 9. *Kampers v. York Fire & Casualty Insurance Company*, 2019 ONCA 56
**Year:** 2019
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/17523/index.do

FACTS: The respondent was injured in a 2005 motor vehicle accident in Nevada and sought benefits under the OPCF 44R Endorsement in her Ontario policy. During a June 2016 pre-trial, both counsel agreed Ontario law applied, but the respondents' counsel later discovered this was contrary to the Court of Appeal's decision in *Chomos*, which held that s. 10 of OPCF 44R makes threshold and collateral benefit issues governed by the law of the place of the accident (Nevada). The appellant argued the parties had formed a binding litigation agreement to apply Ontario law.

ISSUES: The primary issue was whether the parties' agreement at the pre-trial constituted a binding litigation agreement to apply Ontario law, or merely an admission by the respondents' counsel based on a misunderstanding of the law. A secondary issue was whether the motion judge erred in permitting the respondents to withdraw their choice of law admission.

DECISION: The court dismissed the appeal, upholding the motion judge's declaration that Nevada law applies (para 22).

REASONS: The court held that the motion judge's finding that there was no litigation agreement, but rather an admission based on a misunderstanding of the law, attracted deference and should not be interfered with (paras 14-16). The court reasoned that the exchange of correspondence did not reflect a negotiation, and the respondents had no reason to bargain for the application of Ontario law since it would prejudice their interests (paras 15-16). The court further held that signing a pre-trial conference report certificate under r. 50.08(3) merely confirms understanding of the report, not a binding litigation agreement (para 18). The court also upheld the motion judge's discretionary decision to permit withdrawal of the admission (para 20).

OPCF 44R ANALYSIS:
RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation – the case directly applies the court's prior interpretation of s. 10 of OPCF 44R in *Chomos* to determine the applicable law.

PASSAGES:
- Para [6]: The court noted that the appellant's counsel's view that Ontario's threshold and collateral benefits provisions applied was "contrary to this court's decision in *Chomos*," which held that "properly interpreted, s. 10 of the OPCF 44R Endorsement provides that questions of threshold and collateral benefits are issues of liability to be governed by the law of the place where the accident occurred, in this case, Nevada."
- Para [11]: The court noted that the appellant did not challenge the motion judge's finding that "*Chomos* governs this case in the absence of a binding choice of law agreement."

KEY HOLDING ON OPCF 44R (if any): The court affirmed that under s. 10 of OPCF 44R, as interpreted in *Chomos*, threshold and collateral benefit issues are governed by the law of the place where the accident occurred (para 6).

## 10. *Carroll v. McEwen*, 2018 ONCA 902
**Year:** 2018
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/17296/index.do

FACTS: Barbara Carroll was catastrophically injured in a motor vehicle accident and sued the tortfeasors, who had only $1,000,000 in liability coverage through Aviva. The appellants also claimed under Ms. Carroll's OPCF 44R inadequately insured motorist coverage with Pilot. After a jury awarded damages including $2,232,000 for future care costs, the trial judge granted a conditional order assigning Ms. Carroll's future statutory accident benefits (SABs) for health care to Aviva and Pilot if they paid the judgment in full (paras 2-8).

ISSUES: The main issue was whether the trial judge erred in granting the conditional assignment of future SABs under s. 267.8(12) of the Insurance Act, specifically: (1) whether the assignment violated the "matching" principles from Gilbert v. South requiring strict correspondence between SABs and the jury award, and (2) whether the assignment was premature before the judgment was paid (paras 12-13).

DECISION: The court dismissed the appeal from the conditional assignment order and denied leave to appeal the costs order (para 11).

REASONS: The court held that the "silo approach" applies to s. 267.8 as a whole, including assignment provisions, and overruled Bannon and Gilbert to the extent they supported strict matching (paras 37-38). The court emphasized that the endorsement's words "directly or indirectly" import a relaxed causation requirement broader than the "arises out of" language in Amos (paras 30, 37).

## 32. *Soriano v. Palacios*, C42225
**Year:** 2005
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/5208/index.do

FACTS: Five-year-old Daniel Soriano was struck and badly injured by a car owned and operated by Natividad Palacios in the garage of her Montreal residence (para 1-2). Daniel and his family were Ontario residents insured under an Ontario automobile policy issued by Wawanesa (para 3). The Sorianos sued Palacios and Wawanesa in Ontario, but Quebec's no-fault Automobile Insurance Act barred any tort action against Palacios (paras 4-5). Wawanesa moved for summary judgment, which was granted; the motion judge found Quebec substantive law applied and the action was statute-barred (para 5).

ISSUES: Whether the appellants were "legally entitled to recover" damages from Palacios under s. 265(1) of Ontario's Insurance Act, given that Quebec's no-fault legislation precluded any tort claim against her (paras 7-10). The court also considered whether the determination of legal entitlement should be governed by the law of the insurance contract (Ontario) or the lex loci delicti (Quebec), and whether there was discretion to apply Ontario law (paras 11-14).

DECISION: The court dismissed the appeal (para 28).

REASONS: The court held that the appellants' legal entitlement to recover from Palacios must be determined by the law governing the tort action, which is the lex loci delicti—Quebec law—with no discretion to apply Ontario law (paras 12-14). Applying Somersall v. Friedman, the court held that legal entitlement must be assessed as of the time of the accident and in light of all relevant laws; since Quebec's no-fault legislation precluded any tort claim against Palacios from the moment of the accident, the appellants could never meet the "legally entitled to recover" requirement (paras 18-20). The court further reasoned that this conclusion was unaffected by the OPCF 44R endorsement's express Quebec exclusion, which was added for certainty only and did not alter the interpretation of the threshold condition (para 25).

OPCF 44R ANALYSIS:
RELEVANCE: Substantive interpretation — the court discusses OPCF 44R in the context of interpreting the "legally entitled to recover" requirement, which appears in both the mandatory statutory coverage and the optional endorsement.

PASSAGES:
- Para [8]: The court notes that s. 265(1) makes mandatory uninsured automobile coverage, and that an insured has the option of obtaining additional coverage through Endorsement OPCF 44R.
- Para [18]: The court explains that OPCF 44R is the successor to "SEF 44", and that both require a plaintiff to be "legally entitled to recover" damages from an uninsured or underinsured driver as a precondition to recovery under the endorsement.
- Para [25]: The court states that the "legally entitled to recover" concept appears in both the Insurance Act and OPCF 44R, but that OPCF 44R also expressly excludes accidents occurring in Quebec for which compensation is payable under the Quebec Automobile Insurance Act. The court holds that this addition "for added certainty" does not affect the interpretation of the threshold condition, which is in the same terms in both the standard policy and the endorsement.

KEY HOLDING ON OPCF 44R (if any): The court held that the OPCF 44R endorsement's express exclusion of Quebec accidents does not alter the interpretation of the "legally entitled to recover" requirement, which is the same in both the mandatory statutory coverage and the optional endorsement (para 25).

## 34. *Gajraj v. DeBernardo*, C36992
**Year:** 2002
**URL:** https://coadecisions.ontariocourts.ca/coa/coa/en/item/2699/index.do

FACTS: The plaintiffs, Ontario residents, were involved in a motor vehicle accident in New York City when the defendant DeBernardo allegedly struck the plaintiff's car from behind, causing it to hit a third vehicle driven by defendant Manusami. The plaintiffs sued in Ontario, naming their insurer Allstate as a defendant under the underinsured motorist coverage endorsement (OPCF 44R). The defendants moved to stay the action for lack of jurisdiction.

ISSUES: (1) Whether the Ontario Superior Court could assume jurisdiction over the out-of-province defendants under the "real and substantial connection" test; (2) whether jurisdiction should be refused on forum non conveniens grounds; (3) whether the action against Allstate should be severed from the action against the out-of-province defendants.

DECISION: The court dismissed the appeal, affirming the stay of the action against the New York defendants (para 28).

REASONS: Applying the eight-factor test from Muscutt, the court held that while the plaintiff's significant damages in Ontario weighed in favour of jurisdiction, the defendants had no connection to Ontario, there was unfairness to the defendants in assuming jurisdiction, and the international nature of the case weighed against jurisdiction (paras 14-24). The court concluded that the real and substantial connection test was not satisfied and that assuming jurisdiction would violate principles of order and fairness (para 25).

OPCF 44R ANALYSIS:
RELEVANCE: Passing reference — the court discussed OPCF 44R only in the context of whether the claim against Allstate fortified the case for assuming jurisdiction against the New York defendants.

PASSAGES:
- Para [19]: The court noted that s. 10 of OPCF 44R provides that quantum issues are determined by Ontario law while liability issues are determined by the law of the place of the accident, and that s. 4 of Regulation 676 provides for determination by agreement, arbitration, or "a court of competent jurisdiction in Ontario." The plaintiffs argued this required the action against Allstate to proceed in Ontario, supporting jurisdiction over the New York defendants to avoid multiplicity.
- Para [20]-[21]: The court held that the claim against Allstate was "entirely speculative" and "secondary and contingent" to the core claim against the New York defendants, and that jurisdiction over extra-provincial defendants should not be "bootstrapped" by such a claim. The involvement of Allstate therefore did not favour assuming jurisdiction.

KEY HOLDING ON OPCF 44R (if any): The court did not interpret OPCF 44R substantively; it merely noted its terms and held that the contingent claim against the insurer under OPCF 44R did not support assuming jurisdiction over foreign defendants.
