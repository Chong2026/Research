## Q2. Mandamus Refused Because of Justiciability Concerns

Where a mandamus claim meets a justiciability objection, the authorities divide along two lines. The first, anchored in the Supreme Court of Canada and re-stated most recently by the Federal Court of Appeal in *Chad*, treats the absence of a public legal duty owed to the applicant as fatal to relief, and expresses that conclusion in the language of justiciability. The second, exemplified by the Federal Court of Appeal's decisions in *Zaghbib* and *La Rose*, treats a bare finding of non-justiciability as an error of law, or as a conclusion that must be tied to the terms of the governing statute rather than to the political character of the subject matter. The supporting decisions are therefore strongest where the duty relied on is owed to Parliament rather than to the applicant, and weakest where non-justiciability is advanced as a free-standing bar.

### The Supreme Court of Canada

The governing anchor is *Canada (Auditor General) v. Canada (Minister of Energy, Mines and Resources)*, [1989] 2 SCR 49. The Court dismissed an appeal in which the Auditor General had "proceeded by way of a notice of motion of July 5, 1984, seeking an order of mandamus directing the respondents to give the Auditor General free access to the documentation", holding that "s. 7(1)(b) of the Auditor General Act is the only remedy the Auditor General has with regard to the claimed denial of his s. 13(1) rights of access to information" (*Auditor General*, [1989] 2 SCR 49; no paragraph pinpoint is available for these passages). The stated reason is statutory rather than doctrinal: where Parliament has supplied the mechanism for resolving the disagreement, that mechanism is the remedy and mandamus is not available. This is the only Supreme Court authority in the group and, on the hierarchy of authority, it governs.

### The Federal Court of Appeal

*Chad v. Canada (National Revenue)*, 2025 FCA 102 is the clearest modern appellate statement of the supporting position. The Court framed the threshold requirement as follows: "For relief in the nature of mandamus to be granted, there must exist a public legal duty to act" (at para 15). Where that duty is absent, "there is no error in the Federal Court's conclusion that the Notice of Application raised no justiciable issue" (at para 27), and the pleading "does not support an order in the nature of mandamus" (at para 28). *Chad* thus merges the existence of a public legal duty with justiciability, so that the failure of the former is expressed as the failure of the latter.

The contrary and qualifying appellate authority must be weighed alongside it. In *Zaghbib v. Canada (Public Safety and Emergency Preparedness)*, 2016 FCA 182, the Court held squarely that "the Federal Court erred in law when it concluded that Mr. Zaghbib's application for mandamus was not justiciable" (at para 35). The Court of Appeal recorded that the judge's "finding that Mr. Zaghbib's complaint is not justiciable is a function of his conclusion that the decision as to whether or how to investigate is not a decision, determination, order, measure or question arising under the Act" (at para 28) — that is, a conclusion about the reach of the statute, not a categorical bar. The appeal was nonetheless dismissed, the application having become moot once the CBSA decided and closed the file [verify], so the refusal of mandamus survived on a different ground.

*La Rose v. Canada*, 2023 FCA 241 (Rennie J.A.) narrows the leading trial-level authority in the same direction, describing *Friends of the Earth*: "The judicial review application was dismissed on the basis that it did not raise justiciable issues (Friends of the Earth at paras. 46 and 48)", and "[m]andamus was refused because the Minister's obligation under the Act was to file a report to Parliament; no public duty was owed, and it was on this basis that the application was dismissed" (at para 41). The Court's synthesis is explicit: "*Friends of the Earth* does not, therefore, stand for the proposition that all claims addressing climate change are inherently non-justiciable; rather, the application was struck on the basis that the Minister's duties under the KPIA were owed to Parliament and that the legislation did not create a public duty enforceable by mandamus" (at para 42). The Court allowed the appeals in part, granting leave to amend the section 7 Charter claim [verify].

### The Federal Court

Trial-level authority supporting the proposition is more numerous, though a good deal of it rests on the same duty-based footing. In *Canadian Union of Public Employees v. Canada (Minister of Health)*, 2004 FC 1334, Mosley J. confined his reasons to justiciability (at para 4) and held that the claims, "while this application raises important questions, they are of an inherently political nature and should be addressed in a political forum rather than in the courts" (at para 40); with respect to the section 23 obligation, "[a]ny remedy, therefore, with regards to fulfilling the section 23 obligation lies within Parliament and not with the courts" (at para 42), and the issue was "not justiciable, as the process of initiating an investigation and issuing a notice of concern to a province with regard to possible non-compliance with the CHA is a political and policy-oriented one" (at para 44). Mosley J. dismissed the application for declaratory and mandatory relief.

In *Friends of the Earth v. Canada (Governor in Council)*, 2008 FC 1183, Barnes J. refused mandatory relief on the basis that the duties relied on were not justiciable and, alternatively, as a matter of discretion: "Even if I am wrong about the issue of justiciability, I would, as a matter of discretion, still decline to make a mandatory order against the Respondents" (at para 47). The reasons also turn on institutional incapacity, since "an attempt by the Court to dictate the content of the proposed regulatory arrangements would be an inappropriate interference with the executive role" (at para 39), and "[i]f the government cannot be compelled to regulate, it cannot be required to carry out the ancillary duties of publishing, reporting or consulting on the efficacy of such measures" (at para 41). As *La Rose* confirms, this decision must now be read as confined to its statutory context.

In *Cupe, Air Canada Component v. Canada (Labour)*, 2012 FC 1484, Kane J. adopted the reasons of Rennie J. and agreed "that the test for mandamus is not met and the issue is not justiciable" (at para 45), adding that "[t]he subject matter is not appropriate in judicial intervention and in any event the Court lacks the capacity to enforce the matter" (at para 45). The mandamus relief was struck from the notice of application.

*Canadian Committee for a Sustainable Eel Fishery Inc. v. Canada (Fisheries, Oceans and the Canadian Coast Guard)*, 2024 FC 683 is to similar effect. Pamel J. held that "[t]his Court should not get involved in the management and policing of the fisheries, and certainly not seek to manage the Minister in carrying out the statutory powers granted to her by Parliament" (at para 38), struck the corresponding relief measures (at para 39), and characterized the interim relief sought as being "in essence an order in mandamus compelling the Minister to conduct the TAC process in a manner allegedly different from how she is conducting it at present" (at para 46).

*Communications, Energy and Paperworkers Union of Canada v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2013 FC 34 belongs on the same side of the ledger, but on a duty-based rather than a justiciability-based ground. Snider J. observed that if a duty to review existed, "there would be a remedy (such as mandamus or certiorari) available to the Union. In my view, there is no such duty" (at para 36); she found that "[s]ection 26(2.1) does not impose a duty on the Minister to conduct a review upon the request of a third party" (at para 47) and dismissed the application "on the basis that there is no 'decision' or 'matter' that can be challenged by way of judicial review" (at para 48). The ratio is absence of duty and absence of a reviewable decision, not a finding of non-justiciability.

Against this body of trial-level authority sit two decisions in which justiciability was not the obstacle. In *Professional Association of Foreign Service Officers v. Canada (Treasury Board)*, 2026 FC 1136, Azmudeh J. found "that the dispute at issue is public in nature and a mandamus is the only remedy that would provide the relief sought by the Applicant" (at para 40), and refused relief only because steps 1-4 of the *Apotex* test, though met, were not matched by evidence: "The Applicant has successfully framed the TB's behaviour as meeting steps 1-4 of the Apotex test. However, there remains a lack of credible and quantitative evidence demonstrating the ongoing scope of the violation" (at para 5). In *Representative for Children and Youth v. British Columbia (Children and Family Development)*, 2010 BCSC 697, Griffin J. held that the Legislature had not made itself the exclusive forum — "Its intention is not to make itself the exclusive forum for the resolution of disputes in the RCYA; its intention is quite the opposite" (at para 54) — and granted relief: "I find that the requirements of an order for mandamus have been established by the petitioner in this case. Such an order will go against the personal respondents" (at para 116). The suggestion that this decision distinguishes *Auditor General* is not supported by the quoted passages [verify].

### Where the weight lies

The binding authority supports the proposition only in its narrower form. *Auditor General* establishes that where Parliament has provided the remedy, or where the duty is owed to Parliament, mandamus will not issue, and *Chad* confirms that the absence of a public legal duty owed to the applicant defeats relief. But the two most sustained appellate treatments of justiciability as an independent bar point the other way: *Zaghbib* holds that a non-justiciability finding on a mandamus application is an error of law, and *La Rose* confines *Friends of the Earth* to its statute. A court asked to refuse mandamus on justiciability grounds alone, without identifying either a duty owed to Parliament or the absence of a public legal duty owed to the applicant, will be met with *Zaghbib* and *La Rose*. The Federal Court decisions in *CUPE v. Canada (Minister of Health)*, *Friends of the Earth*, *CUPE, Air Canada Component* and *Eel Fishery* remain useful for their outcomes, but their reasoning is, in several instances, in tension with the appellate decisions, and *Cepu* and *Chad* show that relief can be refused on duty and reviewability grounds without any justiciability finding at all.

### Gaps

Appellate authority on this question is thin. Only four decisions above the trial level bear directly on it — *Auditor General*, *Chad*, *Zaghbib* and *La Rose* — and *Chad* and *Zaghbib* pull in opposite directions without having been reconciled. No binding appellate decision squarely resolves whether subject-matter justiciability can independently defeat a mandamus claim in the federal sphere, as opposed to operating through the requirement of a public legal duty. On the provincial side, *Representative for Children and Youth* is a single first-instance decision, and the basis on which it is said to distinguish *Auditor General* cannot be confirmed from the passages available.
